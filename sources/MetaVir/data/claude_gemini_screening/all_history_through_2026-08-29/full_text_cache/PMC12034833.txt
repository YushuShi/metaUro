Evaluating recommendation-based dietary and physical activity strategies for prostate cancer prevention: a target trial emulation in the Health Professionals Follow-up Study
Abstract
The 2018 World Cancer Research Fund/American Institute for Cancer Research recommends sustained strategies of physical activity and diet for cancer prevention, but evidence for long-term prostate cancer risk is limited. Using observational data from 27 859 men in the Health Professionals Follow-up Study, we emulated a target trial of recommendation-based physical activity and dietary strategies and 26-year risks of prostate cancer, adjusting for risk factors via the parametric g-formula. Compared with no intervention, limiting sugar-sweetened beverages showed a 0.4% (0.0%-0.9%) lower risk of lethal (metastatic or fatal) disease and 0.5% (0.1%-0.9%) lower risk of fatal disease. Restricting consumption of processed foods showed a 0.4% to 0.9% higher risk of all prostate cancer outcomes. Estimated risk differences for clinically significant disease were close to null for strategies involving fruits and nonstarchy vegetables, whole grains and legumes, red meat, and processed meat, as well as under a joint strategy of physical activity and diet. Compared with a “low-adherence” strategy, maintaining recommended physical activity levels showed a 0.4% (0.1%-0.8%) lower risk of lethal and 0.5% (0.2%-0.8%) lower risk of fatal disease. Adhering to specific components of current physical activity and dietary recommendations may help to prevent lethal and fatal prostate cancer over 26 years.
Keywords: cancer prevention, causal inference, comparative effectiveness, parametric g-formula, prostate cancer, target trial emulation
Introduction
Prostate cancer is the most common cancer among men in the United States (with an estimated 268 490 new cases per year) 1 and the second-leading cause of cancer death in US men (with an estimated 34 500 deaths in 2022). 1 A key question in preventing prostate cancer, particularly clinically significant disease, is the impact of modifying physical activity and dietary factors (highlighted by the American Society of Clinical Oncology as a top research priority). 2 ‑ 4
Current guidelines from the World Cancer Research Fund/American Institute of Cancer Research (WCRF/AICR) 5 recommend adhering to a comprehensive panel of physical activity and dietary behaviors for primary cancer prevention. No randomized trial has evaluated these recommendations sustained over long periods for prostate cancer prevention, in part because of the large size and long follow-up that would be required, as well as the challenges of implementing such a broad intervention. In the absence of evidence from a trial, these effects need to be estimated from observational studies. 6
Previous observational studies have evaluated some of the recommended behaviors, individually or together as a score, 7 ‑ 15 with some suggesting reductions in risk of total prostate cancer 10 , 11 , 14 and high-grade disease. 8 However, further studies are needed to provide measures of absolute prostate cancer risks under clinically realistic sustained strategies (eg, dynamic strategies in which interventions over time realistically depend on evolving characteristics, ie, exercise based on evolving conditions that may limit physical ability). In addition, there is a need to estimate the joint effects of interventions on multiple behaviors, appropriately adjust for treatment-confounder feedback (which occurs when time-varying confounders [eg, body-mass index, BMI] are affected by previous treatment [eg, physical activity and dietary interventions]), and carefully consider competing events given the long latency periods for prostate cancer incidence and mortality.
In this study, we used observational data from the Health Professionals Follow-up Study (HPFS) to evaluate dynamic physical activity and dietary strategies sustained over long periods for the prevention of clinically significant, potentially fatal prostate cancer.
Methods
Specification of the target trial
We designed this observational analysis to emulate a target trial 16 ‑ 18 (ie, a hypothetical pragmatic trial that would have answered the causal question of interest) of physical activity and dietary strategies for the prevention of prostate cancer outcomes. The key protocol components of this target trial are summarized in Table 1 .

Eligibility criteria for the target trial include men aged ≥ 40 years in 1990 with no history of cancer (except nonmelanoma skin cancer) and no history of conditions that could preclude high levels of physical activity: myocardial infarction, stroke, congestive heart failure, or functional impairment (defined as difficulty climbing a flight of stairs or walking 8 blocks due to a physical impairment).
The physical activity and dietary strategies of interest are:
(1) engage in ≥ 1.25 h/wk of vigorous activity or ≥ 2.5 h/wk of moderate activity or an equivalent combination (ie, ≥ 7.5 metabolic equivalent of task [MET]-h/wk of vigorous or moderate activity);
(2) eat ≥ 5 servings/d of fruits and nonstarchy vegetables;
(3) eat ≥ 3 servings/d of whole grains and legumes;
(4) limit processed foods high in fat, starches, or sugars (hereafter referred to as “processed foods”) to ≤ 1 serving/d;
(5) limit red meat to ≤ 3 servings/wk;
(6) limit processed meat to < 1 serving/wk;
(7) do not drink sugar-sweetened beverages;
(8) do not drink alcohol;
(9) joint intervention on dietary factors (2-8); and
(10) joint intervention on all physical activity and dietary factors (1-8).
Individuals are excused from following the physical activity intervention upon the development of a condition that may preclude high levels of physical activity: cancer (except prostate or nonmelanoma skin cancer), myocardial infarction, stroke, congestive heart failure, or functional impairment. 17 That is, if and when men developed 1 of these conditions over follow-up, they would no longer be expected to engage in ≥ 7.5 MET-h/wk of vigorous or moderate activity and could instead exercise as able. This is a clinically realistic strategy, in contrast to the point interventions evaluated in previous observational studies. 7 ‑ 14 Strategies 1 to 6 are threshold interventions that impose a threshold on the specified behaviors. 19 For example, at each follow-up period under strategy 1, everyone engaging in < 7.5 MET-h/wk of vigorous or moderate activity would need to increase their exercise level to ≥ 7.5 MET-h/wk of vigorous or moderate activity, and anyone engaging in ≥ 7.5 MET-h/wk would not need to modify their exercise levels.
The interventions are based on well-defined 2018 WCRF/AICR physical activity and food-based recommendations for cancer prevention, 5 which are also the actionable ways to target the recommendation to maintain a healthy weight. 20 The intensities of all interventions are selected to match the 2018 WCRF/AICR recommendations 5 as well as recent studies of their operationalization. 15 , 21 ‑ 23 There is currently no standard way to define a target threshold for the recommendation to limit processed foods (previous approaches have defined this based on, eg, tertiles of the distribution of intake in a specific population 21 or an intake of < 1.5 servings/d). 22 , 23 A threshold of < 1 serving/d is defined for practicality for trial implementation and interpretability for patients.
The outcomes of interest are incident (1) total prostate cancer (non-T1a at diagnosis), (2) advanced prostate cancer (T3b/T4/N1/M1 at diagnosis), (3) lethal prostate cancer (the soonest of metastatic prostate cancer at diagnosis or during the follow-up, or death due to prostate cancer during the follow-up), and (4) fatal prostate cancer (death due to prostate cancer during the follow-up).
For each eligible individual, follow-up starts upon strategy assignment (baseline) and ends upon loss to follow-up, death due to other causes, the outcome of interest, or the administrative end of follow-up (June 2016), whichever happens first.
The causal estimands of interest are the intention-to-treat effect of being assigned to the specified strategies and the per-protocol effect of adhering to them. Here, deaths due to other causes are competing events for the prostate cancer outcomes of interest, because individuals cannot develop the prostate cancer outcomes once they have died due to other causes. In the presence of these competing events, we consider 2 estimands 24 : the total effect of the specified strategies on prostate cancer outcomes (through all causal pathways between the interventions and outcomes, including those potentially mediated by death due to other causes) and a controlled direct effect (not mediated by death due to other causes [ie, had nobody died from these causes]). Emulation of the target trial
We explicitly emulated the target trial described above using observational data from the HPFS, an ongoing prospective cohort study of 51 529 US male health professionals aged 40 to 75 years at enrollment in 1986. Participants have reported detailed information on medical diagnoses and lifestyle behaviors at enrollment and every 2 years thereafter, including information on height and weight, cigarette smoking, functional impairment, and chronic diseases. Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening has been reported every 2 years since 1994. Physical activity has been reported every 2 years using a validated questionnaire that includes detailed information on the type, frequency, and intensity of activity. 25 Each activity is assigned a MET value, which is a multiple of resting energy expenditure. We summed MET-h/wk from moderate and vigorous activities. Dietary intake has been reported every 4 years via a validated semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire. 26 , 27 We truncated values of physical activity and dietary intake at the 99th percentile to prevent outliers from having an undue influence on the analysis. Table S1 provides detailed definitions of all physical activity and dietary variables.
Prostate cancer diagnoses were ascertained via self-report on the biennial questionnaires and verified using medical records and pathology reports. In a standardized review, information was extracted on diagnosis date and stage (recorded according to the tumor, node, and metastasis staging system; prioritizing pathological over clinical stage when available). Metastasis to bone or other organs after diagnosis was ascertained using prostate cancer–specific biennial questionnaires sent to all prostate cancer survivors and their physicians. Deaths were ascertained through repeated mailings, calls to nonrespondents, and searches of the National Death Index. Cause of death was determined by an endpoints committee of physicians.
We used the observational data to emulate each protocol component of the target trial as closely as possible ( Table 1 ). We applied all eligibility criteria to men in the HPFS who had information on physical activity, diet (with plausible energy intake [800-4200 kcal/d]), birth date, and other risk factors in 1990 and the preceding questionnaire. Values of physical activity, diet, and other time-varying variables were carried forward until they were updated in a subsequent questionnaire or until nonresponse to an entire questionnaire (which was considered a loss to follow-up). The causal contrast of interest was the observational analogue of the per-protocol effect. We assumed that men were exchangeable at baseline conditional on the covariates specified in Table 2 and adjusted for baseline covariates to emulate randomization. The analysis proceeded as for the per-protocol analysis of the target trial.

Statistical analysis
We used the g-formula, 28 , 29 a generalization of standardization to time-varying treatments and confounders, to estimate 26-year risks of prostate cancer under each physical activity and dietary strategy. In contrast to conventional stratification-based methods for confounding adjustment, the g-formula appropriately handles treatment-confounder feedback, that is, a setting like ours in which the measured confounders (eg, BMI) are affected by prior treatment (eg, physical activity and dietary interventions). The parametric g-formula has been previously applied to estimate the effects of lifestyle interventions on coronary heart disease, 29 ‑ 31 type 2 diabetes, 32 stroke, 33 , 34 asthma, 35 mortality, 17 , 36 and weight gain. 37
We considered 2 approaches to account for death due to other causes as competing events, each of which corresponds to a different causal question. 24 In the primary analysis, we estimated the risk of each prostate cancer outcome that would have been observed had everyone adhered to the physical activity and dietary strategies throughout the follow-up. In this analysis, deaths due to other causes were not defined as censoring events to estimate the total effect of the treatment on the outcome of interest through all causal pathways between treatment and the outcome. In a secondary analysis, we estimated the risk of each prostate cancer outcome that would have been observed had everyone adhered to the physical activity and dietary strategies throughout the follow-up and had no one died of causes other than prostate cancer. In this analysis, deaths due to other causes were defined as censoring events to estimate a (controlled) direct effect of the treatment on the outcome of interest not mediated by death due to other causes.
Our models included the following baseline covariates: age; BMI; smoking history; diabetes; hypertension; hypercholesterolemia; prebaseline physical activity and dietary intake of fruit and nonstarchy vegetables, whole grains and legumes, processed foods, red meat, processed meat, sugar-sweetened beverages, alcohol, and total energy intake; family history of prostate cancer; parental history of myocardial infarction before age 60; and a healthy behavior score (defined as the sum of indicators for regular multivitamin use, routine physical examinations, and screening via rectal examination, sigmoidoscopy, or colonoscopy). Our models also included the following time-varying covariates: BMI; diabetes, hypertension, and hypercholesterolemia (and time since diagnosis for each); physical activity; dietary intake of fruit and nonstarchy vegetables, whole grains and legumes, processed food, red meat, processed meat, sugar-sweetened beverages, alcohol, and total energy intake; and a composite variable for cancer diagnoses (except prostate cancer and nonmelanoma skin cancer), myocardial infarction, stroke, congestive heart failure, and functional impairment (and time since occurrence). All models included indicator variables for period of follow-up, baseline covariates, and time-varying covariates measured in the previous questionnaire. See Table S2 and Appendix S1 for details on the variables and models used.
We compared the estimated 26-year risks had all eligible men followed each of the strategies with the risk under the observed behaviors—that is, under no intervention on physical activity or diet, or the “natural course”—via a risk ratio and a risk difference. 38 In a secondary analysis, we instead considered a “low-adherence” referent group (defined as following behaviors inconsistent with the specified recommendations, eg, < 7.5 MET-h/wk of physical activity). We used nonparametric bootstrapping with 500 resamples to calculate percentile-based 95% confidence intervals for all estimates.
Sensitivity analyses for unmeasured confounding
A key threat to the validity of our estimates is potential confounding by chronic disease severe enough to affect both physical activity (or diet) and risk of prostate cancer outcomes. The g-formula provides a natural way to partly address this issue by estimating the risk under interventions that are only applied at a given time to individuals who are sufficiently healthy at that time. Therefore, in our primary analysis, we considered potential interventions under which men were excused from adhering to the recommended physical activity levels after the development of functional impairment or a serious condition (myocardial infarction, stroke, congestive heart failure, or cancer [except prostate or nonmelanoma skin cancer]) that might limit participation. In sensitivity analyses, we (1) excused men from the recommended dietary interventions, as well as the physical activity intervention, after the development of one of these conditions and (2) expanded the set of serious conditions that excuse individuals from following the physical activity intervention to also include angina pectoris, pulmonary embolism, diabetes, chronic renal failure, rheumatoid arthritis, gout, ulcerative colitis/Crohn disease, and Parkinson disease.
However, the validity of this approach relies on the assumption that the available data at a given time contain sufficient information to identify individuals at that time who are not healthy enough to maintain the recommended behaviors. We therefore also conducted alternative analyses in which all intervention and covariate data (except data on conditions that may preclude high activity) were lagged by 2 years (ie, a minimum latency period analysis of 2 years). 30 We also modified our eligibility criteria to (1) restrict to never-smokers at baseline and (2) restrict to men aged < 80 years at baseline (and excuse them from the physical activity intervention upon age 80). Lastly, to explore the possibility of residual confounding (eg, by underlying health status), we used accidental or injury-related death as a negative outcome control not directly affected by the recommended dietary behaviors but for which the effect of these behaviors might be similarly confounded. 39 We also incorporated cardiovascular death as a positive outcome control.
Other sensitivity analyses
We assessed the robustness of our estimates to various analytic decisions. Specifically, we (1) additionally adjusted for PSA screening, (2) additionally adjusted for time since prostate cancer diagnosis in analyses of lethal and fatal prostate cancer, (3) removed adjustment for total energy intake, (4) adjusted for covariates from the previous questionnaire rather than the questionnaire concurrent with physical activity and diet, (5) specified different functional forms for the covariates, and (6) altered the assumed temporal ordering of covariates when modeling the joint distribution of time-varying covariates reported in the same questionnaire.
Data extraction was performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute), and analyses were performed with R software, version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), including the “gfoRmula” package. 40
The study was approved by the institutional review board of the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health and those of participating registries as required.
Results
A total of 27 859 men were eligible for the study ( Figure 2 ). The baseline characteristics of these men are shown in Table 2 . The median age was 57.0 years, and the median BMI was 25.1 kg/m 2 . The median level of moderate and vigorous physical activity (24.0 MET-h/wk) exceeded the recommended level (7.5 MET-h/wk). However, median levels of dietary intake were less satisfactory, with overconsumption of processed foods (3.0 vs recommended < 1.0 serving/d) and sugar-sweetened beverages (0.9 vs recommended 0 servings/d), as well as insufficient consumption of whole grains and legumes (1.5 vs recommended ≥3 servings/d). Median levels of intake for other dietary components were close to the recommended levels. Over a 26-year follow-up period, 4010 men were diagnosed with total prostate cancer, of whom 303 (7.6%) were identified as having advanced prostate cancer, 426 (10.6%) as having lethal prostate cancer, and 291 (7.3%) as having fatal prostate cancer (see Table S3 for counts of losses to follow-up and deaths in each analysis).

Table 3 shows the estimated 26-year risks of prostate cancer outcomes under the recommendation-based physical activity strategy, joint dietary strategy, and joint physical activity and dietary strategy. Under no intervention, the 26-year risks were 16.4%, 1.24%, 1.87%, and 1.30% for total, advanced, lethal, and fatal prostate cancer, respectively. Estimated risk differences comparing the joint physical activity and dietary strategy with no intervention were 1.87% (95% confidence interval [CI], –0.61% to 4.25%) for total prostate cancer, 0.66% (–0.18% to 1.74%) for advanced prostate cancer, 0.12% (–0.63% to 0.87%) for lethal prostate cancer, and –0.08% (–0.70% to 0.60%) for fatal prostate cancer (see Figure S2 for standardized survival curves). Estimates were similar when comparing the joint dietary strategy with no intervention. Estimated risk differences comparing the physical activity strategy with no intervention were close to null for all 4 prostate cancer outcomes. Approximately 12% of men would have needed to change their behavior in each period, on average, to continue adhering to the physical activity strategy; these proportions were more than 40% for the individual dietary intake strategies and approximately 99% for the joint physical activity and dietary strategy ( Table S4 ). Under the joint physical activity and dietary strategy, the estimated BMI over follow-up was consistent with recommendations to maintain a healthy BMI ( Figure S3 ).

Estimates were similar in secondary analyses that defined deaths due to other causes as censoring events ( Table S6 ). In secondary analyses that compared each strategy with a corresponding “low-adherence” strategy (rather than with the observed behaviors in this population), estimates were similar for the dietary strategies but further from the null for the physical activity strategy (eg, estimated 26-year risk difference –0.40% [95% CI, –0.76% to –0.08%] for lethal prostate cancer and –0.49% [–0.84 to –0.20] for fatal prostate cancer) ( Table S7 ).

Discussion
We quantified the effectiveness of adhering to recommendation-based physical activity and dietary strategies for the prevention of clinically significant prostate cancer using high-quality observational data on 27 859 men with 26 years of follow-up. While we found little indication that a joint strategy of these behaviors substantially influences the incidence of prostate cancer in this population, we detected differences in risk when comparing each component strategy with usual behaviors: limiting sugar-sweetened beverages showed a 1.5% higher risk of total prostate cancer but a 0.4% to 0.5% lower risk of lethal and fatal prostate cancer, limiting alcohol intake showed a 1.2% lower risk of total prostate cancer, and restricting consumption of processed foods showed a 0.4% to 0.9% higher risk for all prostate cancer outcomes. When comparing each recommended strategy with a corresponding “low-adherence” strategy, we also found evidence for potential benefits of maintaining recommended physical activity levels (until it becomes impractical to do so) for the prevention of lethal and fatal prostate cancer (0.4% to 0.5% lower risk), suggesting a greater potential benefit of physical activity in a population with lower background levels of physical activity.
Few observational studies have evaluated the 2018 WCRF/AICR recommendations for prostate cancer prevention. 13 ‑ 15 In a cohort study in the HPFS, each 1-point increase in scores summarizing adherence to these recommendations was associated with neither aggressive prostate cancer (defined as T4/N1/M1 or Gleason score ≥ 8 at diagnosis; hazard ratio, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.94-1.07) nor lethal prostate cancer (hazard ratio, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.96-1.11). 15 In another cohort study among older adults (NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study), each 1-point increase was not associated with advanced prostate cancer. 13 Results for specific components of the recommendations and prostate cancer risk were not reported in either study. 13 , 15 In a case-control study from Spain, each 1-point increase in an adherence score was associated with a 19% lower odds of total prostate cancer (398 cases) and 14% lower odds of high-grade prostate cancer (91 cases). 14 This study also reported a lower odds of total prostate cancer for limiting processed foods, sugar-sweetened beverages, and alcohol, comparing the top vs bottom tertile of each component score. 14 The direction of the finding for alcohol, but not for processed foods or sugar-sweetened beverages, is consistent with our findings for total prostate cancer. However, the interpretation of these studies is not straightforward because physical activity and diet were only measured at a single point in time (and at an unspecified time in the case-control study). Further, the analytic approach of both studies precluded estimation of appropriately adjusted absolute risks. Earlier studies that evaluated the 2007 version of the recommendations for prostate cancer prevention were limited in similar ways. 7 ‑ 12 Outside the context of the WCRF/AICR recommendations, previous analyses in the HPFS have compared relative rates of prostate cancer outcomes among individuals reporting different levels of physical activity 41 , 42 and intakes of fruit and vegetables, 43 whole grains, 44 processed meat, 41 and alcohol, 45 but differences in the exposure definitions limit the comparability of our findings. In the present study, the target trial approach 16 ‑ 18 allowed us to estimate absolute risks under sustained, recommendation-based strategies that realistically depend on evolving clinical characteristics. The magnitude of our estimated risk differences is clinically meaningful, considering randomized trial estimates of a 0.18% lower 16-year risk of prostate cancer mortality among men assigned to PSA screening vs no screening. 46
Our study had several additional strengths. First, the HPFS captures rich longitudinal data on demographic, lifestyle, and clinical features that allowed us to characterize individuals with high resolution and adjust for many potential confounders. Second, the large size of the study population and long follow-up (26 years) allowed us to evaluate the effectiveness of physical activity and dietary strategies with respect to clinically significant prostate cancer outcomes (including 303 advanced, 426 lethal, and 291 fatal prostate cancers). Third, unlike some previous studies on this topic, our analytic approach allowed us to estimate the joint effects of interventions on various behaviors, appropriately adjust for treatment-confounder feedback, and explicitly target 2 estimands of potential interest in the setting of competing events: the total effect and a controlled direct effect. Lastly, we performed several sensitivity analyses to address potential confounding that leveraged excusing criteria as a natural way to address confounding by clinical disease as well as negative outcome controls as a tool for detecting bias in observational studies.
Our study also had some potential limitations. First, as in any observational analysis, we are unable to rule out the possibility of unmeasured or residual confounding. While we adjusted for many potential confounders and our estimates were robust to several sensitivity analyses for potential confounding, the possibility of unmeasured or residual confounding cannot be excluded. For example, confounding by underlying health care–seeking behavior may be of particular concern for the analyses of total prostate cancer, whereby more health-conscious individuals may be more likely to follow a healthy lifestyle and more likely to interact with the health system and be diagnosed with (potentially indolent) disease. 47 ‑ 49 However, our study population was restricted to health professionals, and we adjusted for a baseline healthy behavior score in the primary analysis as well as time-varying measures of PSA screening in a sensitivity analysis. Nonetheless, residual confounding by these factors may help to explain why adherence to several recommended behaviors showed a slightly higher risk of total prostate cancer. Second, we relied on self-reported information, which is subject to measurement error. However, previous validation studies in the HPFS have shown that the physical activity questionnaire provides a useful measure of average weekly activity , 25 and the food frequency questionnaire captures intake of individual foods with reasonable reproducibility and validity. 26 , 27 Finally, our estimates may not be generalizable to other populations with different distributions of physical activity, dietary intake, risk factors, or effect modifiers.
In summary, our findings suggest that adhering to a joint strategy of physical activity and dietary strategies based on the 2018 WCRF/AICR recommendations may not substantially influence the incidence of clinically significant prostate cancer over 26 years but that limiting sugar-sweetened beverages and maintaining recommended physical activity levels (until it becomes impractical to do so) may help to prevent lethal and fatal disease. Future studies of these strategies in populations with different distributions of physical activity and diet are needed.
