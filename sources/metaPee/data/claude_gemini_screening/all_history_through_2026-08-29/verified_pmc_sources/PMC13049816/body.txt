The association between plant protein intake and cancer risk: a prospective cohort study
Background
To examine the association between plant protein intake and the risk of overall and site-specific cancers in a large prospective cohort.
Methods
This prospective cohort study included 192,428 participants from the UK Biobank, recruited from 2006 and followed until 2023. Plant protein consumption information was assessed using validated 24-hour dietary recalls. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association of plant protein consumption with cancer risk were calculated using Cox proportional risk regression models. Nonlinear associations were tested using restricted cubic spline analysis.
Results
During a median follow-up of 11.2 years, 22,779 cancer events were recorded. Compared to participants in the lowest quartile of plant protein intake, those in the highest quartile had HRs for overall cancer, lung cancer, renal cancer, and colorectal cancer of 0.91 (95% CI = 0.88–0.95, P < 0.001), 0.86 (95% CI = 0.73-1.00, P = 0.048), 0.69 (95% CI = 0.55–0.87, P = 0.002), and 0.89 (95% CI = 0.79-1.00, P = 0.046), respectively. Furthermore, each 10% increase in the ratio of plant protein to total daily protein was associated with a 3% lower overall cancer risk (HR = 0.97, 95% CI = 0.95–0.98, P < 0.001). Subgroup analyses suggested that the negative association was more pronounced in male. Among individuals who did not meet the WHO-recommended protein intake, a nonlinear association between plant protein intake and overall cancer risk was observed, characterized by a modest reduction in risk at moderate intake levels with no further risk reduction at higher intake levels ( P for nonlinearity = 0.02).
Conclusion
This cohort study indicates that higher dietary plant protein intake is significantly associated with a reduced risk of overall and specific cancers, including lung, renal, and colorectal cancer. These findings support the notion that increasing the consumption of plant protein-rich foods is beneficial for health.

Keywords
Introduction
Cancer is a major public health problem globally. According to the American Cancer Society estimates, there will be more than 2 million new cancer cases in the United States in 2024 [ 1 ]. Dietary habits are considered to be an important controllable factor for cancer. A number of prospective studies have confirmed that a healthy diet can reduce the risk of certain cancers [ 2 – 4 ]. As a crucial component of the diet, dietary protein plays a vital role in maintaining health [ 5 , 6 ]. Both the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and the World Health Organization (WHO) recommend that adults consume 0.83 g/kg/day to meet their daily needs [ 7 , 8 ].
Beyond total intake, the source of dietary protein has emerged as an important dimension of nutritional quality, with proteins commonly classified as animal- or plant-derived. A growing body of epidemiologic evidence suggests that higher intake of certain animal-protein foods, particularly processed meat, is associated with increased cancer risk [ 9 ]. Meanwhile, plant proteins appear to offer a healthier alternative [ 6 , 10 ]. Several prospective cohort studies have demonstrated that higher plant proteins intake was associated with lower prevent various chronic diseases risks, including cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and chronic kidney disease [ 11 – 14 ]. Plant-protein-rich diets tend to have a lower inflammatory potential and are accompanied by higher intakes of dietary fiber and other bioactive compounds, which may improve metabolic and immune-related pathways relevant to carcinogenesis [ 15 ]. Despite growing interest in plant protein intake, evidence regarding its association with cancer risk remains inconsistent [ 16 ]. For example, Cutler et al. reported an inverse association between dietary flavonoid intake and overall cancer risk in postmenopausal women [ 17 ], whereas Wang et al. and Nozue et al. found no significant associations between intake of flavonoids or fermented soy products and the risk of total or site-specific cancers [ 18 , 19 ]. In addition, several studies focusing on renal cancer reported no significant associations between macronutrient or protein intake and renal cancer risk [ 20 , 21 ]. These studies have several limitations, including relatively small sample sizes, limited follow-up duration, and a focus on specific plant protein–rich foods rather than total plant protein intake. In addition, many studies have been restricted to single cancer outcomes. Therefore, large prospective cohort studies with long-term follow-up and more comprehensive dietary assessments are needed to better clarify the association between plant protein intake and cancer risk.
In addition to protein source, total protein intake may also influence cancer-related biological processes, suggesting that the association between plant protein intake and cancer risk could vary according to overall protein consumption. Experimental studies have shown that prolonged exposure to low-protein diets may inhibit tumor growth and enhance anti-tumor immune responses [ 22 , 23 ], providing a biological rationale for examining plant protein intake in the context of total protein adequacy.
Therefore, using data from the UK Biobank, a large prospective cohort with repeated dietary assessments, this study aimed to evaluate the association between dietary plant protein intake and overall cancer risk, as well as the risk of several site-specific cancers with high incidence, including lung, renal, colorectal, prostate, and breast cancer. Furthermore, we explored potential effect modification by gender and total protein intake level, defined according to the WHO-recommended daily protein intake threshold (0.83 g/kg/day).
Methods
Study population
The UK Biobank is a large prospective cohort comprising over 502,000 individuals aged 37 to 73 years who were recruited from 22 assessment centers across England, Wales, and Scotland between 2006 and 2010 [ 24 , 25 ]. Written informed consent was obtained from all volunteers. The study has received approvals from the North West Multi Centre Research Ethics Committee (reference number 21/NW/0157), the National Information Governance Board for Health and Social Care in England and Wales, and the Community Health Index Advisory Group in Scotland [ 26 ].
Participants will be excluded from this study if they (I) did not complete at least one valid questionnaire before the prevalent malignant cancer diagnosis; (II) completed all questionnaires while on a diet or while sick; (III) consumed either too much or too little total calories in the previous 24 h (male: > 4,200 kcal or < 800 kcal; female: > 3,500 kcal or < 500 kcal); (IV) Weight information was missing. After excluding 309,931 individuals, a total of 192,428 participants were included in the study (Fig. 1 ).

Dietary assessment
Dietary information was gathered using the Oxford WebQ, an online 24-hour dietary recall questionnaire. This web-based 24-hour dietary recall questionnaire includes a list of common foods and 32 beverages in the UK, asking participants to report their servings consumed in the past 24 h (include both weekend days and week days). It has been validated and effectively approximates true dietary intake [ 27 ]. Dietary information was collected five times from April 2009 to June 2012. The first round took place at the assessment center for participants recruited between April 2009 and September 2010, followed by four web-based assessments from February 2011 to April 2012 [ 28 , 29 ]. We utilized estimated nutrient intakes, including plant protein, animal protein, total protein, and total energy intake. These estimated nutrient intakes were automatically calculated by Oxford WebQ using food composition data from the UK Nutrient Databank (UKNDB) (2013) [ 30 ], based on participants’ reported consumption of all food items consumed (protein supplements were not included) within the preceding 24 h [ 31 ]. The nutrient density approach was used to adjust plant protein intake based on total energy intake (per 1,000 kcal), which served as the primary exposure variable in this study [ 32 ]. Additionally, the proportion of plant protein within total daily protein intake (plant protein proportion in daily intake) was also considered. The average value was utilized for participants with multiple valid 24-hour dietary recalls.
Outcome ascertainment
The outcome was the first primary cancers identified by national cancer registries (Health and Social Care Information Centre and the NHS Central Register) and hospital inpatient records before September 11, 2023 [ 33 , 34 ] coded according to ICD-9/10. The Table S1 provides detailed ICD codes for overall cancer (excluding non-melanoma skin cancer) and site-specific cancers. Follow-up lasted from the date of the last valid 24-hour dietary recall until the first occurrence of cancer, death, or the last follow-up date, whichever came first.
Statistical analysis
Participants were categorized into quartiles according to plant protein intake (g/1,000 kcal) and the proportion of plant protein in total daily protein intake. These categorizations were used to facilitate comparisons across intake levels and to assess potential dose–response relationships. In addition, plant protein intake and plant protein ratio were modeled as continuous variables (per 10 g/1,000 kcal and per 10% increase, respectively) to evaluate linear trends. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), we further adjusted for a set of covariates selected a priori based on existing literature and biological plausibility as potential confounders of the association between plant protein intake and cancer risk. These included sociodemographic factors (race, Townsend Deprivation Index [ 35 ] [TDI; an area-based measure of material socioeconomic deprivation derived from census variables, with higher scores indicating greater deprivation; Q1–Q5], education level, and employment status), which are known to influence dietary behaviors and health outcomes [ 36 , 37 ]. Anthropometric and lifestyle-related factors (BMI [kg/m²], physical activity level, smoking status, and alcohol consumption) were included due to their established associations with cancer risk [ 38 ]. Total energy intake (kcal/day) and animal protein intake (g/1,000 kcal/day) were incorporated to account for overall dietary intake and potential substitution effects between plant and animal protein sources. Diabetes status was included as a relevant metabolic condition associated with both diet and cancer risk [ 39 ]. Missing values of covariates were imputed using a random forest algorithm. Covariates with less than 5% missing data were imputed using the random forest method [ 40 ]. Otherwise, missing values were retained and categorized as “unknown” to minimize potential bias. Detailed information on missing values can be found in the Table S2. To explore potential non-linear associations between plant protein intake (or intake ratio) and overall cancer risk, restricted cubic spline models were fitted using the fully adjusted model. In analyses of colorectal cancer, additional adjustments were made for processed meat intake and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug use, given their established relevance to colorectal cancer risk [ 41 , 42 ]. Multicollinearity was evaluated using generalized variance inflation factors (GVIF), confirming no substantial correlation issues among the independent variables [ 43 ]. Detailed covariate definitions are provided in Method S1.
Subgroup analyses by gender were conducted to explore potential differences in effects. In the male subgroup, further analyses were performed for prostate cancer, with additional adjustments for prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing history and marital/cohabitation status, as these factors are associated with prostate cancer [ 44 , 45 ]. In the female subgroup, additional analyses were conducted for breast cancer, with further adjustments for female-specific factors including menopausal status, age at menarche, age at menopause, number of births, age at first birth, history of breast cancer screening, oral contraceptive use, and hormone replacement therapy, all of which are well-established determinants of breast cancer risk [ 45 ]. Female participants were further stratified into premenopausal and postmenopausal groups to assess heterogeneity based on menopausal status. In another parallel subgroup analysis, participants were stratified according to whether they met the WHO-recommended daily protein intake (< 0.83 g/kg/day vs. ≥0.83 g/kg/day), and associations were examined separately within each stratum. Restricted cubic splines were also applied in subgroup analyses to evaluate potential non-linear relationships.
In the sensitivity analysis, we excluded participants who developed cancer within the first two years to eliminate potential reverse causation. In addition, we restricted the analysis to participants who completed at least two 24-hour dietary assessments to minimize random measurement error in plant protein intake (115,667 participants completed at least two questionnaires).
All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0). A bilateral P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Proportional hazard assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, and no violations of these assumptions were detected ( P > 0.05). This study has been reported in line with the STROBE-nut checklist [ 46 ].
Result
Characteristics of study population
The main baseline characteristics of the participants are illustrated in Table 1 according to quartiles of plant protein intake. The baseline characteristics specific to participant gender are shown in Table S3. The mean plant protein intake in the study population was 13.8 g/1,000 kcal/day (median 13.4 g /1,000 kcal/day) and an overall range from 0 to 52.0 g/1,000 kcal/day. During a median follow-up of 11.2 years of 192,428 participants, a total of 22,779 cancer events (excluding non-melanoma skin cancer) were observed, including 5,208 cases of prostate cancer, 4,228 cases of breast cancer, 2,761 cases of colorectal cancer, 1,479 cases of lung cancer, and 727 cases of renal cancer. Participants in the highest quartile (Q4) of plant protein intake were more likely to be female, have a higher level of education, lower BMI, higher physical activity level, less smoking, less alcohol drinking, lower intake of processed meat and animal protein, and were more likely not to meet the WHO recommended daily protein intake for adults.

Association between plant protein intake and cancer risk
The HRs for developing overall cancer compared to the lowest intake quartile were as follows: the second quartile (Q2)-0.96 (95% CIs: 0.92–0.99, P = 0.016), the third quartile (Q3)-0.96 (95% CIs: 0.93–0.99, P = 0.049), and the highest quartile (Q4)-0.91 (95% CIs: 0.88–0.95, P < 0.001). In the model quantifying continuous intake, each 10 g/1000 kcal/day increase in plant protein intake was associated with an 8% lower overall cancer risk (HR = 0.92, 95% CIs: 0.89–0.96, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2 ). Compared with participants in the lowest quartile, those in the highest quartile of plant protein intake was associated with a 14% lower risk of lung cancer (HR Q4 vs. Q1 = 0.86, 95% CIs: 0.73-1.00, P = 0.048). In continuous analyses, each 10 g/1,000 kcal increase in daily plant protein intake was associated with a 17% lower lung cancer risk (HR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.72–0.97; P = 0.020). The negative association between renal cancer risk and plant protein intake also was statistically significant both in the highest intake quartile (HR = 0.69, 95% CIs = 0.55–0.87, P = 0.002) and the continuous model (HR per 10 g/1000 kcal/day = 0.77, 95% CIs = 0.61–0.96, P = 0.022) (Fig. 2 ). In contrast, the association between colorectal cancer risk and plant protein intake was statistically significant only in the highest intake quartile (HR Q4 vs. Q1 =0.89, 95% CIs = 0.79-1.00, P = 0.046) (Table S4).

We further analyzed the relationship between the plant protein ratio in daily protein intake and cancer risk. As shown in Table S5, participants in the highest quartile had a 10% lower overall cancer risk compared to those in the lowest quartile (HR Q4 vs. Q1 = 0.90, 95% CIs = 0.84–0.95, P < 0.001; HR per 10% increase = 0.97, 95% CIs = 0.95–0.98, P < 0.001). However, the negative correlation between specific-sites cancers and plant protein intake ratio was no longer significant in the fully adjusted model (Table S6).
The restricted cubic spline (RCS) curve examination indicated no significant nonlinearity (nonlinearity: P = 0.16 for plant protein intake and P = 0.69 for plant protein ratio in daily protein intake) (Fig. 3 ). We also test for potential nonlinearity in the overall cancer risk for each subgroup (Fig. 4 and Figure S1). Restricted cubic spline analyses revealed a nonlinear association between plant protein intake and overall cancer risk among individuals who did not meet the WHO-recommended protein intake ( P = 0.02 for nonlinearity) (Fig. 4 ), with an inflection point at approximately 16.9 g/1,000 kcal/day. This point fell within the 4th quartile, exhibiting the lowest overall cancer risk in the Cox proportional hazards model (Table 2 ), indicating consistency between the two results.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses
In a gender-specific subgroup analysis, we observed significant differences (Table 2 ). In the male group, plant protein intake was significantly associated with reduced risks of overall cancer (HR Q4 vs. Q1 = 0.88, 95% CIs = 0.84–0.93, P < 0.001), lung cancer (HR Q3 vs. Q1 = 0.79, 95% CIs = 0.64–0.97, P = 0.026), renal cancer (HR Q4 vs. Q1 = 0.61, 95% CIs = 0.45–0.81, P < 0.001), and colorectal cancer (HR Q4 vs. Q1 = 0.79, 95% CIs = 0.68–0.92, P = 0.003). In contrast, among female, a statistically significant inverse association between plant protein intake and overall breast cancer risk was observed only among those with protein intake below 0.83 g/kg/day (HR Q4 vs. Q1 = 0.82, 95% CIs = 0.67–0.99, P = 0.035). For prostate cancer risk, we did not observe evidence of an association with plant protein intake (HR Q4 vs. Q1 = 0.97, 95% CIs = 0.89–1.05, P = 0.466). As illustrated in Table S6, the gender subgroup analysis of plant protein ratio in daily protein intake indicated that a higher ratio was protective against overall (HR Q4 vs. Q1 = 0.95, 95% CIs = 0.93–0.98, P < 0.001), lung (HR Q3 vs. Q1 = 0.75, 95% CIs = 0.58–0.97, P = 0.03) and colorectal cancer (HR per 10% increase = 0.92, 95% CIs = 0.85–0.99, P = 0.019) in male. However, for female, this protective effect was significant only for breast cancer (overall: HR Q4 vs. Q1 = 0.71, 95% CIs = 0.53–0.96, P = 0.028 and post menopause: HR Q3 vs. Q1 = 0.73, 95% CIs = 0.54–0.99, P = 0.044) among those individuals whose protein intake is below the standards recommended by the WHO.
In subgroup analyses stratified by total protein intake relative to the WHO–recommended level (0.83 g/kg/day), higher plant protein intake was associated with lower overall cancer risk both below and at or above recommended levels (Q4 vs. Q1: HR < 0.83 g/kg/day = 0.88, 95% CI = 0.82–0.95, P = 0.001; HR ≥ 0.83 g/kg/day = 0.94, 95% CI = 0.90–0.99, P = 0.012). This association remained statistically significant when plant protein intake was modeled as a continuous variable (Table 2 ). For lung cancer, a significantly negative correlation with daily plant protein intake was found only among participants with a daily protein intake exceeding 0.83 g/kg/day (HR Q3 vs. Q1 = 0.82, 95% CIs = 0.69–0.97, P = 0.019; HR per 10 g/1000 kcal/day = 0.81, 95% CIs = 0.67–0.98, P = 0.028). In those with a daily protein intake of below 0.83 g/kg, we observed that the highest quartile of plant protein intake was negatively associated with the risk of renal cancer (HR = 0.61, 95% CIs = 0.41–0.91, P = 0.016).
In our analysis of plant protein intake rates, we observed a similar trend. Specifically, among male, higher plant protein intake ratio was associated with significantly lower risks of overall cancer (HR Q4 vs. Q1 = 0.85, 95% CIs = 0.79–0.93, P < 0.001; HR per 10% increase = 0.95, 95% CIs = 0.93–0.98, P < 0.001), lung cancer (HR Q3 vs. Q1 = 0.75, 95% CIs = 0.58–0.97, P = 0.03), and colorectal cancer (HR per 10% increase = 0.92, 95% CIs = 0.85–0.99, P = 0.019). In contrast, among female, higher plant protein intake was significantly associated with lower risks of breast cancer (overall: HR Q4 vs. Q1 = 0.71, 95% CIs = 0.53–0.96, P = 0.028; post menopause: HR Q3 vs. Q1 = 0.73, 95% CIs = 0.54–0.99, P = 0.044) only among those with total protein intake below 0.83 g/kg/day (Table S6).
In sensitivity analyses excluding participants who experienced outcome events within the first two years of follow-up, most associations remained unchanged. However, the association between plant protein intake and lung cancer risk was attenuated, and the association with colorectal cancer was no longer statistically significant (Table S7-10). In analyses restricted to participants who completed at least two 24-hour dietary assessments, the associations between colorectal cancer risk and breast cancer risk lost statistical significance which may be due to loss of participants and cases (Table S11-14).
Discussion
In this cohort study in the UK population, higher plant protein intake and a higher proportion of plant protein in total protein intake were associated with a significantly lower risk of overall and site-specific cancers. In subgroup analyses, the negative association between plant protein intake and cancer risk was more pronounced in males. Furthermore, evidence indicates that higher plant protein consumption can reduce overall cancer risk in both high and low total protein intake populations. Our findings emphasize the importance of introducing more plant protein-rich foods into the daily diet, serving as a vital guide for dietary choices for the general public.
Previous prospective studies have focused on single plant protein sources, such as soy or nuts, yielding inconsistent conclusions [ 19 , 47 ]. Our study demonstrates an 9% lower risk of overall cancers among participants who consumed the highest amount of plant protein per day compared to those who consumed the least. Moreover, there was an 8% lower overall cancer risk for every 10 g/1,000 kcal increase in plant protein intake. These findings support the role of plant proteins in cancer prevention. Unlike animal proteins, plant proteins do not enhance the body’s inflammatory response, which is responsible for cellular damage that drives the development of many cancers [ 48 , 49 ]. Recent studies have also found that plant protein extracts derived from legumes can effectively inhibit MMP-9 activity, a critical role in colorectal cancer progression and metastasis [ 50 , 51 ]. Furthermore, certain plant protein sources can also regulate hormone levels in the body. For example, some nuts and beans rich in plant protein contain estrogen-like components (like phytoestrogens) that may inhibit cancer development [ 49 , 52 – 54 ]. Additionally, plant protein sources are often abundant in dietary fiber, improving intestinal microecology and accelerating the excretion of carcinogens [ 55 ]. These findings may explain the relationship between higher plant protein intake and lower overall cancer risk. We also considered the potential influence of lower animal protein intake, as participants who consumed higher amounts of plant protein tended to have lower intake of animal protein. with regard to this, animal protein intake was adjusted for in all fully adjusted models, and restricted cubic spline analyses also revealed a linear association between the proportion of plant protein intake and overall cancer risk ( P for nonlinearity = 0.69). These results indicate that the protective effect of plant protein is independent of the relatively reduced intake of animal protein.
We further analyzed the association between plant protein and site-specific cancer risk. Our study revealed a significant negative association between plant protein intake and the risk of lung, renal and colorectal cancers. Previous studies have already shown that soy protein intake has a positive effect on respiratory cancer [ 56 ], while a high proportion of animal protein in the diet may increase the risk of respiratory cancer [ 57 ]. In light of our study, it can be hypothesized that the relationship between plant protein intake and lung cancer risk may be influenced by the source of plant protein and its proportion within total protein intake. It is worth noting that a higher proportion of participants in the highest quartile (Q4) of plant protein intake had total protein intake below the WHO-recommended level. This distribution may partly explain why, for certain outcomes such as lung cancer, the strongest protective association was observed in the third quartile (Q3), particularly among participants with adequate total protein intake (≥ 0.83 g/kg/day). In support of this heterogeneity, restricted cubic spline analyses among participants with total protein intake below the WHO-recommended level suggested a nonlinear association, characterized by a modest reduction in risk at moderate levels of plant protein intake with no further risk reduction at higher intake levels (Fig. 4 c). At the same time, Q4 generally showed the lowest or comparable risk estimates for overall cancer and other site-specific cancers, suggesting that a dietary pattern characterized by relatively lower total protein intake with a higher emphasis on plant-derived protein may also be associated with favorable cancer outcomes. Taken together, these findings raise the possibility that not only the source of protein, but also the balance between total protein quantity and protein source, may be relevant to cancer risk. However, given the observational nature of this study, whether lower total protein intake combined with a higher proportion of plant protein should be recommended warrants further investigation. While most existing studies have reported no association between plant protein intake and renal cancer risk [ 20 , 21 ], the borderline significance identified in our study suggests that a potentially protective effect of plant protein intake on renal cancer risk may require a certain intake threshold to manifest. Two prospective cohort studies have shown that replacing animal protein with equivalent amounts of plant protein can reduce the risk of colorectal cancer [ 58 , 59 ]. This is consistent with our research findings. We also find that higher plant protein intake was associated with a lower risk of breast cancer in specific subgroups rather than uniformly across all female. Notably, the inverse association was observed primarily among female with total protein intake below the WHO-recommended level, particularly for postmenopausal breast cancer. This pattern suggests that the potential protective role of plant protein intake for breast cancer may depend on the broader dietary background, including total protein intake, and may be more relevant in hormonally sensitive settings such as post menopause [ 60 ]. In contrast, we found no evidence of an association between plant protein intake and prostate cancer risk, which is consistent with findings from previous meta-analyses and prospective studies [ 61 ].
We identified a significant effect of gender on the relationship between plant protein consumption and cancer risk. Specifically, in the male group, the highest quartile of plant protein intake was associated with a notable lower risk of overall cancer, as well as lung, renal, and colorectal cancer. In contrast, in female participants, this negative association was not observed. This difference may be attributed to variations in genetic, hormonal, and lifestyle factors between the genders [ 62 ]. Some immune genes located on the inactivated X chromosome can escape inactivation in female compared to male, leading to overexpression of immune genes in female lymphocytes. Additionally, T-cell counts tend to be higher in female than in male [ 63 , 64 ]. These differences may enable male, who generally have a more “weakened” immune system, to derive relatively greater benefits from increased consumption of plant proteins. In addition, research indicates that hormones can also significantly influence the development of cancers in non-reproductive tissues, including lung, renal, and colon cancers [ 62 ]. For example, in the development of lung cancer, female hormones have been found to cause a decrease in the activity of tobacco carcinogen-degrading enzymes, thereby increasing the risk of lung cancer [ 65 ], which may counteract the protective effects of plant proteins in the female population. Differences in lifestyle habits between genders are also a factor to be considered, as we observed that the physical activity levels of male are higher than those of female, which may enhance the efficiency of plant protein utilization in male compared to female [ 66 ]. Furthermore, the observed difference in plant protein intake between male and female may be partly due to the higher total calorie intake in male, which allows them to consume greater absolute amounts of plant protein. Energy-adjusted absolute intake measures (g/1,000 kcal) may therefore better capture associations with cancer outcomes than relative intake measures, although no clear non-linear threshold was observed in sex-stratified spline analyses.
Strengths and limitations
We conducted a comprehensive analysis by considering plant protein as a proportion of total protein intake, fully accounting for differences in protein sources and total intake among individuals. This study not only examined overall cancer as the primary outcome but also included several site-specific cancers with high incidence rates. Our research provides a realistic and comprehensive evaluation of the role plant proteins play in cancer prevention. The large sample size, sufficient endpoints, and long-term follow-up enhance the robustness of our findings.
There are also some potential limitations to our study. First, we were unable to establish an exact causal relationship, as people who consume higher amounts of plant protein are likely to adopt healthier lifestyle practices, which may also reduce cancer risk. Additionally, the results may be confounded by some unknown confounders. Second, dietary information was collected using a web-based 24-hour dietary recall questionnaire, which may be subject to recall and reporting biases. Third, our study was a prospective cohort analysis conducted within a British population. Although the sample size was substantial and representative, the results may not be fully recapitulated by other ethnic or regional populations. Finally, the mechanisms underlying the effects of plant proteins on specific cancer risks remain inadequately understood. While evidence indicates that plant proteins may reduce inflammatory responses, inhibit tumor cell growth, and regulate hormone levels, these mechanisms require further experimental studies. In this study we did not differentiate and examine the relationship between specific plant protein sources and cancer risk, and we hope that this will be explored in depth in subsequent studies.
Conclusion
This large cohort study reveals that a higher intake of plant proteins is negatively associated with the risk of overall cancer, as well as lung, renal, and colorectal cancer. We also observe that this negative correlation is more pronounced in males. Additionally, we found that an increase in the proportion of plant proteins exhibits a protective effect on cancer incidence, regardless of whether total protein intake meets the WHO recommendations. The findings of this study provide valuable guidance for public health initiatives and individual dietary choices.
