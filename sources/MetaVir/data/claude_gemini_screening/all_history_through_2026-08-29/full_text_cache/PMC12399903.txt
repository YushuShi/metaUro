Dairy-Derived Bioactive Peptides and the Risk of Prostate Cancer among Iranian Men: A Case-Control Study
This case-control study aimed to assess the association of dairy-derived digestion-resistant bioactive peptides (BPs) with the risk of prostate cancer (PCa) in a cohort of 50 men with PCa and 99 controls. Dietary, demographic, and anthropometric factors of all participants were assessed. Various types of dairy-derived BPs were determined by an in silico method. High intake of total digestion-resistant BPs, low molecular weight peptides, high molecular weight peptides, phosphorylated residues, disulfide bonds, and antioxidant peptides was not significantly associated with the risk of PCa in the adjusted models ( P >0.05), while low-fat [odds ratio (OR): 0.19, 95% confidence intervals (CI): 0.05－0.66] and total dairy intake (OR: 0.23, 95% CI: 0.06－0.79) were inversely associated with PCa risk. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to report an association between dairy-derived BPs and the risk of PCa and an inverse association with consumption of dairy products in Iranian men likely due to the low intake of this population. Notably, the BP content of dairy products was not significantly associated with PCa risk.
INTRODUCTION
Digestion-resistant bioactive peptides (BPs) obtained from dairy products exert cholesterol-lowering, anticancer, and antihypertensive effects after absorption into the circulation ( Barati et al., 2020a ; Auestad and Layman, 2021 ). Fermentation can effectively increase the content of BPs in dairy products ( Barati et al., 2020b ). The functions of BPs have been mainly investigated by in vitro , in vivo , and short-term randomized controlled trials, while relatively few long-term observational studies have assessed the association between BPs and the risk of chronic diseases, must likely due to the lack of quantitative methods to measure BPs in foods ( Parastouei et al., 2023 ). The recent development of a time-saving and cost-effective in silico method has enabled assessment of the long-term health effects of BPs in foods with the use of observational studies ( Barati et al., 2020a ). However, studies investigating the link between BP intake and chronic disease risk using this method are still limited. Notably, a cohort study reported a direct association between BP consumption and an increased risk of hypertension (HTN), marking the first indication of the potentially harmful effects of long-term intake of digestion-resistant BPs due to disruption of endocrine or enzymatic systems ( Barati et al., 2021b ).
Prostate cancer (PCa) is a leading cause of cancer-related death among American men ( Ahad et al., 2024 ). However, recent observational studies have yielded conflicting results regarding the link between dairy intake and PCa risk ( Willett and Ludwig, 2020 ; Mikami et al., 2021 ). Most studies examining the relationship of milk and dairy consumption with PCa risk have been conducted in developed countries with high per capita dairy intake ( Zhao et al., 2023 ), leaving a gap in research from developing countries.
Although BPs, calcium, lactose, and fat of milk and dairy products are the most important components, the mechanisms explaining the beneficial or harmful effects of long-term milk and dairy consumption on human health remain unclear. While research on dairy-derived calcium and fat is extensive ( Aune et al., 2015 ; Preble et al., 2019 ; Ashtary-Larky et al., 2021 ; Orlich et al., 2022 ), no observational studies have specifically examined the relationship between dairy-derived BPs and PCa risk.
Long-term consumption of high amounts of dairy products may increase or decrease the risk of PCa ( Oczkowski et al., 2021 ; Zhao et al., 2023 ). Therefore, clarification of the association of the components of dairy products and the risk of PCa could provide helpful insights. Although the association of dairy fat, calcium, and lactose intake with PCa risk has been widely studied, this retrospective study is the first to investigate the link between dairy-derived digestion-resistant BPs and PCa risk in Iran, a country with low per capita dairy consumption.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
The cohort of the present case-control study included 51 PCa patients (age, 40－78 years) and 104 controls (age, 43－71 years) randomly sampled from patients admitted to the same hospitals for various medical issues, including eye or nose disorders, skin diseases, fractures and sprains, trauma and injuries, and various illnesses other than acute, non-neoplastic conditions or diet-related chronic diseases during the same time period. The inclusion criteria for the case group were histologically confirmed PCa and no history of cancers at the other anatomical sites. All participants were admitted to the Labbafi-Nejad and Baqiyatallah hospitals in Tehran, Iran. The case and control groups were matched based on body mass index (BMI) (<19, 19－25, 25－30, and >30 kg/m 2 ) and age (5-year groups). Socio-demographic and lifestyle information (age, smoking status, family history of cancer, and personal history of chronic diseases) was collected with a general questionnaire. Measurements of weight and height were performed while the participants were wearing only light clothing without shoes. Height was recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm using a portable stadiometer, while weight was recorded to the nearest 0.1 kg using a digital scale (Seca GmbH). BMI was calculated as body weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Waist circumference was recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm using a non-stretchable measuring tape. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Committee of Baqiyatallah University of Medical Sciences (approval no. IR.BMSU.REC.1399.567) and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects described in the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to inclusion in this study, informed consent was obtained from all participants.
Determination of various types of digestion-resistant BPs
Dairy-derived digestion-resistant BPs were quantified as described previously ( Barati et al., 2020a ; Parastouei et al., 2023 ). In brief, dietary intake was assessed using a valid and reliable semiquantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) covering the past year ( Mirmiran et al., 2010 ). The dietary intake of BPs was estimated based on the recorded dairy food items in the FFQ. To calculate the intake of digestion-resistant and BPs, the consumption of dairy items (g) was multiplied by the digestion-resistant BPs content of these items, as described in our previous in silico study ( Barati et al., 2020a ). In brief, the “enzyme action” tool from the BIOPEP webserver ( http://www.uwm.edu.pl/biochemia/index.php/pl/biopep ) was used to simulate proteolysis of dairy proteins. First, three enzymes (trypsin, pepsin, and chymotrypsin A) proteolyzed the target proteins via in silico digestion. Then, to characterize the active peptides, the fragments obtained from proteolysis of αs1-casein, αs2-casein, β-casein, κ-casein, β-lactoglobulin, and α-lactalbumin were submitted to the BIOPEP database.
To estimate the dietary intake of BPs from dairy products, we considered all dairy-related food items from the FFQ list, which included fat-free milk, low-fat milk, high-fat milk, cocoa milk, strained yogurt, low-fat yogurt, high-fat yogurt, doogh, cheese, traditional ice cream, industrial ice cream, butter, cream, and kashi. The bovine milk protein sequences (excluding the signal peptide) were individually analyzed through in silico digestion. The BIOPEP tool was used to predict the peptide sequences produced by proteolytic enzymes ( Minkiewicz et al., 2008 ). Furthermore, the active fragments of the theoretically generated peptides from bovine milk proteins were identified with the BIOPEP tool. In silico digestion of each milk protein was conducted separately to identify (1) total peptide fragments, (2) peptides with biological activity, (3) numbers of dipeptides, tripeptides, tetrapeptide, pentapeptides, hexapeptides, heptapeptide, and longer peptides, (4) anti-diabetic peptides, (5) antihypertensive peptides, and (6) antioxidant peptides.
Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 15.0, SPSS Inc.). Participants were categorized based on tertiles of consumption of different types of digestion-resistant BPs. Linear regression and chi-square tests were employed to analyze trends in continuous and categorical variables across these tertiles. A multivariable logistic regression model was used to predict the risk of PCa across the tertiles of different types of digestion-resistant BPs. In addition, the odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. The first tertile of each type of digestion-resistant BP was considered as the reference category. A logistic regression model was adjusted for potential confounders, including BMI, age, total energy intake, smoking status, and family history of cancer. A probability ( P ) value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
RESULTS
Characteristics of the study participants
In total, 51 patients with PCa and 104 age- and BMI-matched controls were enrolled in the study. One patient from the case group and five from the control group were excluded because of incomplete dietary data. Finally, data from 50 PCa patients and 99 controls were included for statistical analyses. The demographic characteristics and dietary intake of the 50 PCa patients and 99 controls are shown in Table 1 . Waist circumference and intake of dairy-derived disulfide bonds were higher in the case group than the control group ( P <0.001 and P =0.030, respectively). Also, the proportions of smokers and participants with a family history of cancer were higher in the case group ( P <0.001 and P =0.025, respectively). As shown in Table 1 , there was no significant difference in the consumption of dairy products and dairy-derived BPs based on PCa status ( P >0.05).
Association of dairy-derived BPs with PCa risk
The association between dairy-derived BPs and the risk of PCa based on the size, function, and posttranslational modifications of the peptides is reported in Table 2 . Overall, the risk of PCa was not associated with consumption of dairy-derived antioxidants, anti-diabetic, high molecular weight (HMW), low molecular weight, and anti-HTN peptides, phosphorylated residues, disulfide bonds, and total digestion-resistant BPs. The association remained nonsignificant even after adjusting for age, BMI, total energy intake, smoking status, and family history of cancer ( P for trends>0.05).
Association of dairy-derived calcium, and lactose intake with risk of PCa
As shown in Table 3 , there was no association between the consumption of low-fat, high-fat, and total dairy products, dairy-derived calcium or lactose and the risk of PCa in the crude models. However, the association between the consumption of low-fat (OR: 0.19, 95% CI: 0.05－0.66) and total dairy (OR: 0.23, 95% CI: 0.06－0.79) was significant after adjusting for age, BMI, smoking status, family history of cancer, and total energy intake.
DISCUSSION
The association between the consumption of milk and dairy products and the risk of PCa has been widely investigated, although the findings have been contradictory. This case-control study was designed to assess the association between the consumption of dairy and dairy-derived BPs and the risk of PCa among Iranian men. To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the first to investigate the link between food-derived BPs and PCa risk, and the first to assess this relationship in Iranian men.
In the present study, the consumption of total dairy was indirectly associated with PCa risk, while subgroup analysis found an indirect association between the consumption of low-fat dairy products and PCa risk. Many cohort and case-control observational studies, in addition to a systematic review, have reported a positive association of the consumption of milk and dairy products with an increased risk of PCa, which was attributed to the fat content of dairy products ( Sargsyan and Dubasi, 2021 ; Zhao et al., 2023 ). Milk contains 70 to 75 g of saturated fatty acids (SFAs) per 100 g of total fat, which is relatively high as compared to other food items ( Givens, 2017 ) and often considered harmful. A cohort study with a 9-year follow-up of 23,281 patients with PCa revealed that dietary SFAs significantly increased the risk of both advanced and fatal PCa ( Pelser et al., 2013 ). Most mycotoxins are lipophilic and hardly soluble in water ( Sotnichenko et al., 2019 ). Therefore, reducing the fat content of dairy products can decrease toxicity ( Šimko and Kolarič, 2022 ). The link between mycotoxins and cancer risk has been extensively documented ( Claeys et al., 2020 ). Moreover, reducing the dairy fat content may lower levels of other cancer risk factors, such as insulin-like growth factor 1 ( Marín-Quiroga et al., 2015 ). While most observational studies attribute the increased PCa risk to dairy fat, some have reported a positive association with the consumption of low/non-fat dairy products ( Mikami et al., 2021 ). This suggests that in addition to dairy fat, calcium and digestion-resistant BPs may also contribute to the association between dairy consumption and PCa risk.
Milk and dairy products are among the most important dietary sources of digestion-resistant BPs. Although many short-term studies have shown the beneficial effects of BPs on human health, relatively few observational studies have investigated the long-term health effects ( Barati et al., 2021a ). For example, a case-control study reported a protective effect of digestion-resistant BPs against the risk of breast cancer ( Jabbari et al., 2022 ). Conversely, a prospective study observed a hypertensive effect associated with these peptides ( Barati et al., 2021b ). In the current study, there was no meaningful association between the consumption of dairy-derived BPs and the risk of PCa. Nonetheless, further investigations are warranted to clarify the mechanisms underlying the direct and indirect associations between the consumption of dairy-derived BPs and PCa risk, and potential implications in public health.
Recent research has proposed potential mechanisms of the effects of BPs on cancer-related pathways. Studies have demonstrated that the anticancer effects of BPs derived from food proteins can influence various cellular processes, including apoptosis, cell proliferation, and angiogenesis ( Cicero et al., 2017 ; Deng et al., 2023 ). These mechanisms imply that despite our findings not reaching statistical significance, there could be underlying biological trends that warrant further investigations. Additionally, the impact of BPs can vary greatly depending on structure and the specific type of cancer ( Cicero et al., 2017 ; Daliri et al., 2017 ; Hamley, 2017 ). For example, some peptides have been linked to decreased tumor growth and metastasis in animal models ( Ghadiri et al., 2024 ), indicating that these pathways should be further investigated, even when human studies do not yield significant statistical results ( Daliri et al., 2017 ). Our intention was to enhance the understanding of the intricate interactions between BPs and cancer development while remaining closely aligned with existing evidence. Dairy-derived BPs have been shown to inhibit growth of cancer cells ( Jabbari et al., 2022 ). However, there are concerns regarding potential impacts on cancer incidence due to possible disruptions to endocrine and enzyme systems, which might contribute to the heightened cancer risk associated with dairy-derived BPs ( Barati et al., 2021a ). Furthermore, most food-derived peptides, including those from dairy, are classified as HMW peptides with low absorptive potential in the gastrointestinal tract. Consequently, metabolism of HMW peptides is largely mediated by the intestinal microbiome. Microbial catabolism of food-derived peptides produces a wide range of metabolites, which may have detrimental effects ( Barati et al., 2021b ), thereby highlighting the complexity of the interactions among dairy-derived BPs, the microbiome, and the resulting metabolic byproducts, underscoring the need for further research to elucidate these relationships and potential implications for health, particularly concerning cancer incidence.
Other than total dairy intake and BPs, the associations of dairy-derived lactose and calcium with PCa risk were also assessed. The results showed no meaningful association. To the best of our knowledge, no similar studies have assessed the association of calcium and lactose from dairy products and the risk of PCa. However, a prospective study with a 24-year follow-up showed that the positive association of dietary calcium intake (more than 2,000 mg/d) and PCa risk was blunted after adjustment for dietary phosphorous intake ( Wilson et al., 2015 ).
The current case-control study found no meaningful association between dairy-derived BPs and the risk of PCa in Iranian men. However, the per capita intake of dairy products is relatively low in this population. Given the observed indirect association between low-fat dairy intake and PCa risk, it can be concluded that even among individuals with low overall dairy consumption, higher consumption of low-fat dairy products (not necessarily total dairy intake) may reduce the risk of PCa. Nonetheless, further studies with larger sample sizes are necessary to clarify these findings and better understand the relationship between dairy consumption and PCa risk.
There were some limitations to the current study that should be addressed. First, case-control studies have limited capacity to assess the association of FFQ-derived variables and the risk of PCa. Second, since PCa is characterized by slow growth and can take years to detect, employing an FFQ that only considers the past year may not be suitable to establish a link between the consumption of BPs and the risk of PCa. Further matching for dietary habits and socioeconomic-related variables is suggested for future case-control studies. Finally, although current guidelines recommend the consumption of three or more servings of milk and dairy products per day, Willett and Ludwig (2020) stated that pending further research, guidelines for milk and dairy products should ideally recommend only two servings per day.
