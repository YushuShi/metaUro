Dietary Fiber Intake and Risk of Advanced and Aggressive Forms of Prostate Cancer: A Pooled Analysis of 15 Prospective Cohort Studies

Background
Evidence of an association between dietary fiber intake and risk of advanced and aggressive forms of prostate cancer (PC) and PC mortality is limited.
Objective
To examine associations between intakes of dietary fiber overall and by food source and risk of advanced and aggressive forms of PC.
Design
Pooled analysis of the primary data in 15 cohorts in three continents. Baseline dietary fiber intake was assessed using a validated food frequency questionnaire or diet history in each study.
Participants/setting
842,149 men were followed for up to 9–22 years between 1985–2009 across studies.
Main outcome measures
The primary outcome measures were advanced (stage T4, N1, or M1 or PC mortality), advanced restricted (excluded men with missing stage and those with localized PC who died of PC), high grade (Gleason score ≥8 or poorly differentiated/undifferentiated) PC, and PC mortality.
Statistical analysis
Study-specific multivariable hazard ratios (MVHR) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression and pooled using random effects models.
Results
Intake of dietary fiber overall, from fruits, and from vegetables was not associated with risk of advanced (n=4,863), advanced restricted (n=2,978), or high-grade PC (n=9,673) or PC mortality (n=3,097). Dietary fiber intake from grains was inversely associated with advanced PC (MVHR comparing the highest vs. lowest quintile=0.84, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.76–0.93), advanced restricted PC (MVHR=0.85, 95%CI 0.74–0.97), and PC mortality (MVHR=0.78, 95%CI 0.68–0.89); statistically significant trends were noted for each of these associations (p≤0.03), while a null association was observed for high grade PC for the same comparison (MVHR=1.00, 95%CI 0.93–1.07). The comparable results were 1.06 (95%CI 1.01–1.10, p-value, test for trend=0.002) for localized (n=35,199) and 1.05 (95%CI 0.99–1.11, p-value, test for trend=0.04) for low/intermediate grade (n=34,366) PC.
Conclusions
Weak nonsignificant associations were observed between total dietary fiber intake and risk of advanced forms of PC, high grade PC, and PC mortality. High dietary fiber intake from grains was associated with a modestly lower risk of advanced forms of PC and PC mortality.
INTRODUCTION
Prostate cancer (PC) is the second most common cancer (excluding non-melanoma skin cancer) in men worldwide, accounting for 14% of all diagnosed cancers. 1 In high-resource areas, current estimates for the five-year survival for early-stage PC is almost 100%, 2 – 5 which is partly attributable to the high prevalence of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening for PC and subsequent diagnosis of earlier stage disease. 6 In contrast, the five-year survival for advanced and metastatic PC is low at 30%. 2 As risk factors for advanced or aggressive PC may differ from indolent PC, 7 , 8 identification of factors related to risk of advanced and aggressive PC and PC mortality is warranted to help reduce PC morbidity and mortality as advanced and aggressive PC have the greatest lethal potential. 9
Many factors are hypothesized to contribute to the development and progression of PC, including modifiable factors such as diet. 10 In particular, high dietary fiber intake has been suggested to reduce low grade inflammation, improve insulin sensitivity, and lower insulin like growth factor (IGF1) concentrations, all of which may reduce PC risk. 11 Yet, the few epidemiologic studies that have examined associations between dietary fiber intake and risk of advanced and aggressive PC and PC mortality 12 – 17 have yielded inconsistent results. Accordingly, the 2018 Continuous Update Project (CUP) by the World Cancer Research Fund and American Institute for Cancer Research (WCRF/AICR) concluded that the evidence of an association for dietary fiber and sources of dietary fiber with PC risk is limited and inconclusive. 18 Thus, in 15 cohort studies in the Pooling Project of Prospective Studies of Diet and Cancer (DCPP), 19 the associations between consumption of total dietary fiber, dietary fiber from food sources (e.g., dietary fiber from grains), whole grain foods, and refined grain foods and risk of advanced and high-grade PC and PC mortality were evaluated. Results for total, localized, and low-grade PC are presented for comparison. Only a few of the 15 studies in the current analysis had previously published their findings regarding these associations. 12 – 15
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Study Population
The Pooling Project of Prospective Studies of Diet and Cancer (DCPP) is a large, international consortium of prospective cohort studies that has collected data on dietary factors, anthropometric factors, other cancer risk factors and cancer outcomes. 19 These analyses were conducted using data from 15 cohort studies from North America, Europe, Australia, and Asia ( Table 1 ) 13 – 15 , 20 – 30 that met the following pre-defined inclusion criteria: 1) used a prospective design, 2) had at least one publication on an association between any dietary factor and any cancer, 3) used a comprehensive assessment of long term dietary intake, 4) completed a validation study of the dietary assessment method or a closely related dietary instrument, and 5) included at least 50 incident PC cases ascertained during follow-up. Participants in the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, and Ovarian Cancer Screening Trial (PLCO) were limited to those in the screened arm because the FFQ was only administered at baseline to the screened arm. 23 The questionnaires for the Netherlands Cohort Study were only processed for cases and a random sample of the cohort; thus it was analyzed as a case-cohort study. 21
The Harvard TH Chan School of Public Health Institutional Review Board determined that the research conducted within this consortium is “not human subject research as defined by DHHS and FDA regulations” (protocol number: 10426–104). In addition, each cohort received approval by their respective Institutional Review Board and those of participating registries as required. Across the studies in the consortium, participants either directly provided informed consent or informed consent was implied through completion of study questionnaires by participants.
Dietary Assessment
Dietary information was collected in each cohort at baseline using detailed self-administered food frequency questionnaires (FFQs) or diet histories generally inquiring about the last year; 19 except for some centers in the European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition (EPIC) cohort, 31 which used interviewer-administered quantitative dietary questionnaires 13 ( Supplementary Table 2 ). Intakes of total dietary fiber ( Table 1 ) and dietary fiber from fruits, vegetables, and grains ( Supplementary Table 3 ) were calculated by each study using their own nutrient database; energy-adjusted intakes were calculated using the residual method. 32 Whole-grain foods were defined as foods having >50% whole-grain content (e.g., all-bran cereal, whole-grain bread, and brown rice); all remaining grain foods (e.g., white bread, pizza, pasta, and baked goods) were classified as refined grains. Validation studies were conducted for the FFQ used in each study or a closely related instrument. 33 – 46 Correlation coefficients comparing total dietary fiber intake estimated by the dietary questionnaires to intake estimated from multiple days of dietary records or 24-hour recalls generally exceeded 0.60 across studies ( Supplementary Table 2 ).
Non-Dietary Factors
Information on many non-dietary factors was collected at baseline. Age, height, weight, and smoking habits were assessed in each study. At least 10 studies collected information on race and ethnicity, education, marital status, personal history of diabetes, multivitamin use, and physical activity; seven studies inquired about family history of PC (see Supplementary Table 4 for study availability). The percentage of missing data for each covariate was generally less than 8% in each study.
Case Ascertainment
Incident PC cases were identified in each study using self-administered follow-up questionnaires with subsequent medical record review, linkage with cancer registries or both methods. 13 – 15 , 20 – 30 Some studies also linked to death certificates to identify PC death. 14 , 15 , 20 , 23 , 26 , 27 , 29 , 30 For the Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial (PCPT), 22 cases included only PC diagnosed by a biopsy performed due to elevated PSA or abnormal digital rectal exam (“for cause”).
The primary outcomes examined were advanced and aggressive forms of PC defined as: advanced PC (T4, N1, or M1 tumors, or death where PC was the underlying cause of death), advanced restricted PC (excluded advanced cases who were first diagnosed with localized cancer or had missing stage information at diagnosis who died during follow-up and PC was the underlying cause of death), high-grade PC (tumors with Gleason scores ≥8 or poorly differentiated/undifferentiated) and PC mortality (where the underlying cause of death was PC). These outcomes were selected because they have the greatest lethal potential. 9 Secondary outcomes evaluated for comparison included total PC, localized PC (T1/T2 and N0M0 tumors), and low/intermediate-grade PC (tumors with Gleason score <8 or well/moderately differentiated).
Exclusions
After applying the exclusion criteria used by each cohort, participants with a history of any cancer at baseline, except for non-melanoma skin cancer, and those with energy intakes outside of three standard deviations from the study-specific log e -transformed mean energy intake were further excluded. For analyses of specific PC outcomes, studies were excluded if they contributed less than 50 cases of that outcome.
Statistical Analysis
Total dietary fiber intake and dietary fiber intake from fruits, vegetables, and grains were modeled according to study-specific quintiles as well as using categories defined by common absolute intake cutpoints (e.g., <15 g/day, 15-<20 g/day, etc. for total dietary fiber); the cutpoints in the latter analyses were selected to maximize the exposure range evaluated and ensure adequate case numbers in most categories for most cohorts. For a specific exposure (e.g., dietary fiber from grains), the same reference group was used across all PC endpoints. When at least one of the studies did not have any cases in the lowest intake category or several studies had limited numbers of cases in the lowest intake category for any PC outcome, the lowest intake category was not used as the reference group. For analyses of cancer incidence, follow-up time was calculated from the date of the baseline questionnaire to either the date of PC diagnosis, death, loss to follow-up, or administrative end of follow-up, whichever occurred first. For the PC mortality analyses, follow-up time was calculated from the date of the baseline questionnaire to the date of death, loss to follow-up, or administrative end of follow-up, whichever came first. The primary analytic approach used a two-stage process. 19 Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to calculate study-specific hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). All models were stratified on age at baseline (years) and the year in which the baseline questionnaire was returned. Study center was included as an additional stratification variable for the EPIC study to account for the study design. To control for potential confounding, established PC risk factors and other potential confounding variables (see Table 5 footnote for the list of confounding variables and their categorizations) were included either directly in the multivariable model or using the propensity score method for cohorts with <200 cases of the PC outcome being evaluated. 47 An indicator variable for missing data was created for each covariate.
Study-specific results were combined using a random effects model to calculate pooled HRs. 48 The presence of heterogeneity in the effects between studies was evaluated using the Q and I 2 statistics. 48 , 49 A contrast test 50 was used to assess whether associations for each dietary fiber intake variable differed by PC stage and grade.
Linear trends across categories of each exposure were assessed by including the median value for the corresponding category for each participant as a continuous variable in the model. The Wald test for the coefficient representing the continuous term was used to calculate the P-value for linear trend.
To evaluate whether the associations for each dietary factor and risk of total, advanced, and advanced restricted PC, PC mortality, and high grade PC were linear, the model fit including linear and restricted cubic spline terms selected by a stepwise regression procedure was compared with the model fit with only the linear term using the likelihood ratio test. 51 If the assumption of linearity was met, additional analyses were conducted in which intake was modeled as a continuous variable.
Mixed-effects meta-regression analyses were used to evaluate whether the associations with advanced, advanced restricted, and high-grade PC and PC mortality varied by age, geographical region, follow-up time, body mass index, smoking status, alcohol intake, and multivitamin use. 52 In sensitivity analyses, pooled estimates were calculated 1) after excluding the two studies (PLCO and PCPT) in which all participants received PSA testing as part of their study protocol; 2) after excluding participants with a history of diabetes at baseline; and 3) among US studies in which follow-up time did not extend past 1998 when folate fortification of grain products became mandatory 53 because a U-shaped relationship may exist between folate intake and PC risk. 54 Associations also were examined using a more restrictive definition for high grade PC that excluded poorly differentiated tumors with Gleason scores <8 or tumors with missing Gleason score.
Measurement error corrected risk estimates were calculated using the method of regression calibration for advanced PC, advanced restricted PC, PC mortality, and high-grade PC. 55 – 57 As the validity of dietary fiber intake from fruits, vegetables, or grains was not evaluated in most studies, the data collected on total dietary fiber intake in the validation studies were used as a proxy for the validity of dietary fiber intake from specific food sources.
For all tests, two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SAS software, Version 9.4 of the SAS System for Unix (Cary, NC). 58
RESULTS
During follow up of 842,149 men in 15 cohorts, 52,683 were diagnosed with incident PC. Among the PC cases, 4,863 were advanced tumors, 2,978 advanced restricted tumors, 3,097 PC deaths, and 9,673 high-grade tumors. Median dietary fiber intake varied over 4-fold across the studies ( Table 1 ). Similarly, large variations in dietary fiber intake from grains, fruits and vegetables were observed across studies ( Supplementary Table 3 ).
As the age-adjusted results were generally similar to the multivariable results, only multivariable results are discussed in the text. Overall, no statistically significant associations were observed for total dietary fiber intake and any PC outcome when dietary fiber intake was modeled as quintiles ( Table 5 ). Across all PC outcomes, the pooled multivariable HRs ranged from 0.93 to 0.98 for comparisons of the highest with lowest quintile of intake. Similarly, no significant results were observed when intake was categorized using common absolute intake cut points across studies, except for localized PC, which showed a modest inverse association, although the trend was not statistically significant (p-value, test for trend=0.57, Supplementary Table 6 ).
When dietary fiber intake was examined separately by food source, statistically significant inverse associations were observed between dietary fiber intake from grains and risk of advanced PC, advanced restricted PC, and PC mortality; the pooled multivariable HRs ranged from 0.78 to 0.85 for comparisons of the highest versus lowest quintile ( Table 5 ). For the same comparison, statistically significant 5%-6% increases in risk of localized and low/intermediate-grade PC were found for dietary fiber intake from grains. When dietary fiber intake from grains was categorized using common absolute cut points ( Supplementary Table 6 ), a statistically significant inverse association was observed for PC mortality (pooled MVHR comparing ≥8g/day vs 2-<4g/day = 0.86, 95% CI 0.75–0.98; p-value, test for trend=0.03); a statistically significant inverse trend also was observed for advanced PC (p=0.04). No statistically significant associations were seen for dietary fiber intake from fruits and dietary fiber intake from vegetables and risk of any of the advanced forms of PC ( Table 5 and Supplementary Table 6 ), although weak, statistically significant inverse associations were observed for dietary fiber intake from vegetables and risk of localized and low-grade PC for comparisons of the highest vs lowest quintile ( Table 5 ).
There was no evidence of heterogeneity in the results between studies for intake of dietary fiber overall or from fruits, vegetables, and grains and any outcome ( Table 5 and Supplementary Table 6 ). Participants in PLCO and PCPT received PSA testing as part of their protocol; in sensitivity analyses, associations for advanced PC, advanced restricted PC, and PC mortality were examined after excluding these studies. Results (not shown) were similar to the main results. Analyses were also conducted using a more restrictive definition of high-grade PC that did not include poorly differentiated tumors with Gleason score <8 or those with missing Gleason score for the five studies (CLUE II, CPS II, EPIC, MEC, PLCO) that had Gleason score data available. For intakes of dietary fiber overall and by food source, the results (not shown) for this more restrictive definition did not differ materially from those presented for high-grade tumors.
Nonparametric regression analyses showed that the association between total dietary fiber intake and PC mortality was nonlinear (P-value, test for nonlinearity = 0.02). Therefore, dietary fiber intake was modelled as a categorical variable in analyses examining whether the associations between dietary fiber intake and risk of advanced PC, advanced restricted PC, PC mortality, and high-grade PC varied by age at diagnosis, geographical region, follow-up time, BMI, smoking status, alcohol intake, and multivitamin use. Evidence of effect modification was not observed ( Supplementary Table 7 ).
Whether the inverse associations observed for dietary fiber intake from grains and risk of advanced PC, advanced restricted PC, and PC mortality were modified by study and participant characteristics was also evaluated; for comparison, these analyses were also conducted for high-grade PC. The associations between dietary fiber intake from grains and risk of advanced PC, advanced restricted PC, and high-grade PC and PC mortality were consistent with linearity (P-value, test for nonlinearity >0.05). When dietary fiber intake from grains was modeled using a continuous term, statistically significant 5%-7% lower risks for a 5 g/day increment were observed for advanced PC and PC mortality and a nonsignificant 5% lower risk was shown for advanced restricted PC ( Supplementary Table 8 and Figure 1 ). A null association was observed for high-grade PC. Associations between dietary fiber intake from grains and risk of advanced PC, advanced restricted PC, PC mortality, and high-grade PC generally did not vary by geographical region, BMI, smoking status, alcohol intake, and multivitamin use. Statistically significant interactions were observed for multiple PC outcomes only for age at diagnosis and follow-up time ( Supplementary Table 8 ) with statistically significant inverse associations noted only among men who were at least 65 years of age at diagnosis and when follow-up was 5 years or longer for both advanced PC and PC mortality (p-value, test for interaction ≤ 0.03). When men with a history of diabetes at baseline were excluded from the analyses, similar results to the overall findings were observed for total dietary fiber intake overall ( Supplementary Table 7 ) and dietary fiber intake from grains ( Supplementary Table 8 ).
After correcting for measurement error (estimated using total dietary fiber intake as a proxy for dietary fiber intake from grains, fruit, and vegetables), the associations between dietary fiber from grains and advanced PC, advanced restricted PC, and PC mortality were slightly stronger than the uncorrected estimates ( Supplementary Table 9 ). The pooled age- and energy-adjusted HRs (95% CI) for a 5g/d increment in intake of dietary fiber from grains were 0.93 (0.90–0.97), 0.93 (0.89–0.98), and 0.91 (0.86–0.95) in the uncorrected analyses, 0.90 (0.84–0.96), 0.90 (0.84–0.97), and 0.86 (0.79–0.93) after correction for measurement error for advanced PC, advanced restricted PC, and PC mortality, respectively. For the remaining associations, the uncorrected and measurement error corrected HRs were only slightly different from each other, and none were statistically significant.
In analyses of grain foods, no statistically significant associations were observed for intakes of refined grain foods with PC risk (Supplementary Table 10). Intake of whole grain foods showed nonsignificant modest inverse associations for risk of advanced PC (pooled MVHR comparing ≥150 g/d vs <5g/d=0.90, 95% CI 0.78–1.03), advanced restricted PC (pooled MVHR =0.83, 95% CI 0.65–1.05), and PC mortality (pooled MVHR=0.87, 95% CI 0.74–1.01).
Folate fortification of grain products has been mandatory in the US since 1997, in Australia since 2009, and in the UK, a region in the EPIC study, since 2009. 53 To address concerns that the associations observed for dietary fiber intake from grains and whole grain intake may reflect an association with folate intake, analyses were conducted in which follow-up was limited to the period before the mandatory folate fortification of grain products. These analyses were limited to the US studies (identified in Table 1 ), the only studies with extensive follow-up during both the pre- and post-fortification periods. The associations for intake of dietary fiber from grains ( Supplementary Table 11 ) and whole grains ( Supplementary Table 12 ) and risk of advanced PC and PC mortality in analyses limited to the pre-fortification period were attenuated compared to the results for those same studies covering the entire follow-up period. When whole-grain food intake and dietary fiber intake from grains were mutually adjusted, the results for both dietary fiber from grains and whole grain foods were essentially unchanged (results not shown).
DISCUSSION
In this analysis of participant-level data from 15 prospective cohort studies including over 842,000 men and over 52,000 incident PC cases, there was no evidence of an association for intakes of total dietary fiber, dietary fiber from fruits, and dietary fiber from vegetables with risk of several clinically relevant PC outcomes including advanced, advanced restricted, and high-grade PC and PC mortality. Modest, statistically significant inverse associations for dietary fiber intake from grains with risk of advanced PC, advanced restricted PC, and PC mortality were observed; weaker positive associations were noted for localized and low/intermediate-grade PC. The associations for total dietary fiber intake and dietary fiber intake by food source were similar regardless of whether intake was modeled using study-specific quintiles or categories defined by common absolute intake cut points.
Dietary fiber has been hypothesized to lower PC risk by several potential mechanisms possibly by reducing systemic inflammation, 59 improving insulin sensitivity 60 and decreasing IGF-1 concentrations. 61 Yet, in this study, null associations were observed between total dietary fiber intake and risk of nearly all PC outcomes evaluated. Only one other cohort study 16 that was not included in this pooled analysis was identified that has examined dietary fiber intake in relation to PC risk. That study found an inverse association with total PC risk; advanced stage PC, high-grade PC, and PC mortality were not evaluated. 16 The nonsignificant results observed here for dietary fiber intake from fruit and vegetables agree with prior findings on fruit and vegetable consumption in the DCPP 62 and other prospective studies 63 that showed no significant protective associations between fruit and vegetable consumption and PC risk.
Unlike the null associations noted for dietary fiber intake overall and from fruits and vegetables, a significant modest inverse association was shown for dietary fiber intake from grains and risk of advanced PC, advanced restricted PC, and PC mortality. In addition to two studies 17 , 64 included as part of the EPIC study in our analysis, we are only aware of one additional cohort study that has examined associations between dietary fiber intake from grains and PC risk; that cohort only examined total PC and reported a nonsignificant 5% reduction in risk per 1 standard deviation increase in dietary fiber intake from grains (HR=0.95, 95% CI 0.80–1.12). 16 As grains contain many nutrients with potential anticarcinogenic properties (e.g., minerals, vitamins, and other phytochemicals), 65 , 66 the inverse associations observed for dietary fiber intake from grains with advanced forms of PC in this study may be due in part to these other nutrients rather than dietary fiber itself. Yet, the findings for dietary fiber intake from grains were similar after adjustment for whole-grain consumption. Further, nonsignificant associations were observed for whole-grain foods and risk of advanced forms of PC. Other cohorts also have not observed significant inverse associations for whole grain consumption and PC risk. 67
Additionally, the possibility that the inverse association observed for dietary fiber from grains may be due to more intensive PSA screening cannot be ruled out as PSA testing has been associated with higher cereal intake. 68 Higher PSA testing would result in greater detection of indolent disease, namely, localized and low-grade PC and as expected, a slightly higher risk of these two outcomes was shown for the highest compared to the lowest consumers of dietary fiber from grains. On the other hand, cooked vegetable, salad/raw vegetable, and fresh fruit consumption has also been shown to be positively associated with PSA screening, 68 which would suggest that dietary fiber intake from fruits and vegetables would show similar associations as dietary fiber intake from grains. However, dietary fiber intake from fruits and vegetables was not associated with risk of any advanced form of PC in this study. This pooled analysis included studies in Australia (MCCS), Europe (ATBC, COSM, EPIC, NLCS), and Japan (JPHC I and II) where PSA screening was not adopted as early as in the US and is not as common as in the US. 69 Yet, inverse associations for dietary fiber intake from grains and advanced PC, advanced restricted PC, and PC mortality in the US and other regions were observed, albeit the estimates were slightly stronger in the US studies. As such, although the associations observed are likely not entirely driven by PSA screening, this possibility cannot be excluded as data on PSA screening were not available in most cohorts nor in the data repository of the consortium.
Major strengths of this study are the prospective design, which helped to avoid selection and recall bias, and the large sample size obtained by pooling 15 cohort studies. This allowed for inclusion of studies from different geographical regions including Asia, Australia, Europe, and the US, examination of a wide range of dietary fiber intake across diverse populations, and examination of associations with advanced forms of PC, high grade PC, and PC mortality with high precision. Moreover, harmonizing and standardizing the primary dietary components, covariates, and outcomes and using similar analytical approaches and models reduced sources of heterogeneity between these studies. The large size of these pooled analyses also allowed us to evaluate whether associations differed by geographical region, PC risk factors, and follow-up time.
Despite these strengths, this study had some limitations. Even though geographically diverse, the study population was primarily composed of White men (> 90%); thus, preventing examination of whether associations differed by race or ethnicity. Additionally, the impact of PSA screening and treatment were not able to be accounted for in the advanced PC and PC mortality analyses as these data were not available in most studies. Dietary intake was measured at baseline using a food frequency questionnaire or diet history and is subject to error likely resulting in underestimation of the strength of the association. Furthermore, we were unable to estimate the influence of measurement error directly for dietary fiber from grains. However, when total dietary fiber was used to approximate the measurement error for the assessment of dietary fiber intake from grains, appreciable changes in the HR estimates were not observed. Further, as only data collected at study enrollment were analyzed, changes in intakes over time or across the life course could not be assessed and data on potential confounding factors including PSA screening during follow-up were not available.
CONCLUSIONS
This pooled analysis of 15 prospective cohort studies found no substantial associations between total dietary fiber intake and risk of PC overall, for subtypes defined by stage and grade, and PC mortality. Analyses by food source showed a modest association between high intake of dietary fiber from grains and lower risk of advanced forms of PC. Given that the study population primarily included White men, confirmation of these findings in other racial and ethnic groups is needed.

RESEARCH SNAPSHOT
Research question:
Is consumption of dietary fiber overall or by food source associated with risk of advanced and aggressive forms of prostate cancer?
Key findings:
In a pooled analysis of 15 prospective cohort studies including 842,149 men among whom 52,683 were diagnosed with incident prostate cancer during follow-up, no substantial associations were observed between pre-diagnostic intakes of dietary fiber overall, dietary fiber from fruits, and dietary fiber from vegetables and risk of advanced and aggressive forms of prostate cancer. Higher pre-diagnostic dietary fiber intake from grains was associated with a modestly lower risk of advanced stage prostate cancer and prostate cancer mortality. No evidence of a lower risk was observed for localized and low/intermediate grade prostate cancer.
