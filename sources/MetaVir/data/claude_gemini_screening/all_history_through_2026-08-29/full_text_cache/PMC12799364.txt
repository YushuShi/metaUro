Cancer incidence and mortality risk: associations with diet quality scores in a predominantly low-income and Black cohort study
STS and HMM contributed equally to this work.
Background
Diet quality is a modifiable risk factor for cancer incidence and mortality. However, a substantial research gap exists regarding diet quality metrics’ relationships with cancer risk among Black individuals or those with low socioeconomic status.
Objectives
This study aimed to investigate the associations of dietary approaches to stop hypertension (DASH), empirical dietary inflammatory pattern (EDIP), and empirical dietary index for hyperinsulinemia (EDIH) scores with total, colorectal, prostate, and breast cancer incidence and mortality within the prospective Southern Community Cohort Study (SCCS).
Methods
The SCCS enrolled 84,507 adults, focusing on Black individuals and those with low socioeconomic status. DASH, EDIP, and EDIH scores were calculated based on a food frequency questionnaire and analyzed using cohort-specific quartiles. Chi-square and analysis of variance tests were used to test univariate associations between dietary scores and baseline characteristics. Cox proportional hazards models with adjustment for confounders were used to derive hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Spearman correlation tests were performed to calculate the correlations between dietary scores and biomarkers of inflammation and metabolic dysregulation (e.g., C-peptide, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein).
Results
Diet quality was better in females and White individuals compared with males and Black individuals. Generally, DASH, EDIP, and EDIH showed mostly null or modest associations with cancer incidence and mortality. Notably, higher diet quality measured by DASH was associated with lower colorectal (HR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.65, 0.97) and prostate cancer mortality (HR = 0.56; 95% CI: 0.38, 0.83) among White participants. Overall, Pearson’s correlation test did not show any strong correlations between the biomarkers and dietary scores.
Conclusions
Our study suggests that dietary indices are at most weakly related to cancer risk among the generally low-income SCCS participants. It highlights the need for more research on nutrition and cancer prevention in socioeconomically and racially diverse populations.

Introduction
Diet quality is a modifiable risk factor for cancer [ 1 ], the second leading cause of death in the United States [ 2 ]. Dietary pattern scores are tools that summarize intake patterns based on measures of diet quality. Several dietary pattern scores have been evaluated for association with health outcomes, particularly cancer incidence and mortality [ 3 , 4 ]. Index-based scores, such as the dietary approaches to stop hypertension (DASH) [ 5 ], were developed to reflect the scientific evidence available on diet-disease risk associations, initially for cardiovascular disease but have now also been associated with cancer risk [ [6] , [7] , [8] , [9] ]. Empirical hypothesis-oriented approaches, such as the empirical dietary inflammatory pattern (EDIP) score and the empirical dietary index for hyperinsulinemia (EDIH), were developed using exploratory data methods as well as knowledge about biological pathways to calculate dietary scores [ 10 , 11 ]. Inflammation, insulin resistance, and diets high in fruits and vegetables have all been linked to cancer pathways or risk [ [12] , [13] , [14] , [15] ]. The DASH, EDIP, and EDIH scores were selected for their complementary ability to capture various aspects of diet quality: overall healthy eating patterns (DASH), inflammation (EDIP), and insulin regulation (EDIH), thus providing a broad assessment of diet quality in our cohort. Thus, evaluating DASH, EDIP, and EDIH may be informative for possible relationships between patterns or pathways with cancer risk. Although existing research has linked poor EDIP and EDIH scores to elevated risks of prostate [ 16 ] and colorectal cancers [ [17] , [18] , [19] , [20] ], predominantly in cohorts of White individuals and those with higher socioeconomic status (SES), a substantial research gap persists regarding the relationship between these scores and the dietary patterns of Black individuals or those with low SES.
There is very limited literature on the relationship between DASH, EDIP, and/or EDIH among Black individuals or individuals with low SES whose diets may substantially vary from the typical United States patterns. Racial groups, lower SES, and residing in the Southern United States have been associated with lower diet quality and higher cancer incidence [ [21] , [22] , [23] , [24] ]. The Southern Community Cohort Study (SCCS) is a large prospective cohort based in the Southern United States for which the majority of the study population are Black individuals or low income. Thus, in our study, we examined the associations between DASH, EDIP, and EDIH dietary scores and total, colorectal, prostate, and breast cancer incidence and mortality to determine whether these dietary patterns are associated with cancer risk among all SCCS participants and whether there were differences in associations by race.
Methods
In the SCCS, 84,507 adults aged 40–79 were enrolled between 2002 and 2009 in 12 Southeastern states in the United States. Participants were enrolled in person at one of 71 community health centers (CHC, 85%) or by mailings to stratified random samples of the general populations (GP) of these states [ 25 , 26 ]. Participants completed an interviewer-administered or self-administered survey to ascertain information on demographic, socioeconomic, and anthropometric characteristics, personal and family medical history, and lifestyle choices such as alcohol use and diet. In our study, participants were asked, "What best describes your race or ethnic background?" Participants selected from the following response options: “White,” “Black /African American,” “Hispanic/Latino,” “Asian or Pacific Islander,” “American Indian or Alaska Native,” and “Other.”
Periodic direct contact with participants and linkages with the National Death Index were used to determine vital status and cause of death. Cancer incidence was ascertained through linkages with cancer registries from the 12 Southeastern states. The study was approved by the institutional review boards of Vanderbilt University and the Meharry Medical College, and participants gave written informed consent at study enrollment.
Participants completed a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) containing 89 food and 5 alcohol questions, which asked about the frequency of consumption of certain types of foods and alcohol over the last year [ 27 , 28 ]. Data for participants aged 30–84 living in the South census region from the NHANES and the USDA Continuing Survey of Food Intakes by Individuals were used to estimate portion size and calculate grams per day for each food item [ 29 ].
DASH, EDIP, and EDIH dietary scores were calculated for participants who completed ≥79 of the 89 FFQ questions and had energy intake between 600 and 8000 kcal/d ( N = 77,815). The DASH score was calculated by linking the FFQ data with the MyPyramid Equivalents Database (MPED, version 2.0) [ 30 ] to determine equivalent intake in cups or ounces per 1000 kcal for food groups in the USDA Dietary Guidelines for Americans [ 31 ]. MPED was used as the SCCS FFQ was based on NHANES-III and data collection began in 2002. Components of the DASH score were calculated for fruit, vegetables, grains, dairy, plant-based proteins, meats/fish, sweets, fats and oils, and sodium. The DASH total score ranged from 0 to 90, and a higher score reflected better diet quality. For calculation of the EDIP and EDIH scores, the grams per day values obtained during the FFQ scoring process were converted to the serving sizes as described by Tabung et al. [ 10 , 11 ]. The total number of servings for each component was calculated and multiplied by the weights provided. For EDIH components, the total number of servings was converted to servings per 1000 kcal using the estimated total daily food energy intake before being multiplied by the weights. The components were summed to get the EDIP and EDIH scores. The EDIP score was divided by 1000 to decrease the magnitude of the scores. For EDIP and EDIH, if 3 or more components were missing, the total score and the 18 components for that score were set to missing. Higher values of EDIP and EDIH reflect a poorer quality diet of greater inflammatory and hyperinsulinemic potential, respectively.
After excluding those who reported having had missing dietary data ( N = 6693), cancer other than nonmelanoma skin cancer at baseline ( N = 5014), and those who died <1 month after enrollment ( N = 16), 72,784 participants were available for the analyses ( Supplemental Figure 1 ). Participants with missing data for the EDIP ( N = 93) and EDIH ( N = 235) scores were excluded from the respective analyses. In analyses of cancer risk, participants who were diagnosed with cancer <1 month after enrollment were excluded. In analyses of cancer mortality, participants with missing cause of death were excluded. The dietary scores were analyzed using quartiles derived from the entire cohort, and linear trends in the scores were assessed by treating the quartiles as a linear variable using the median value for each quartile.
To further evaluate the correlation between diet quality and cancer risk, existing biomarker data indicating inflammation or metabolic dysregulation were used to correlate with the dietary scores. These biomarkers were measured from blood and urine samples collected at enrollment in previous studies [ 32 , 33 ]. Seven biomarkers were used for this analysis: C-peptide, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), IL-1β, IL-6, urinary prostaglandin E2 metabolite (PGE-M), and TNF-α. Plasma hsCRP levels were measured using the C-reactive protein (HS) Wide Range kit (Pointe Scientific) on the ACE Clinical Chemistry System (Alfa Wassermann, Inc.) following the manufacturer’s protocol, with a lower detection limit of 0.1 mg/L, as previously outlined [ 32 , 34 ]. Urinary PGE-M (11 alpha-hydroxy-9, 15-dioxo-2, 3, 4, 5-tetranor-prostane-1,20-dioic acid) levels were measured via a liquid chromatography/tandem mass spectrometric method, also described previously [ 35 ]. The lower limit of detection of PGE-M was in the range of 40 pg. Plasma levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were measured using the MILLIPLEX MAP Human High Sensitivity T Cell Panel Premixed 21-plex Immunology Multiplex Assay kit on a Luminex 2000 Analyzer, following the manufacturer’s protocols. The minimum detection levels for the cytokines ranged from 0.11 to 8.17 pg/mL, with mean intraassay and interassay coefficients of variation (CVs) of 6.75% and 9.86%, respectively. HbA1c levels were assessed using the Bio-Rad Variant II Hemoglobin Testing System (HPLC) with the 270–2101 NU kit in the Clinical Chemistry laboratories at Vanderbilt University Medical Center, following the manufacturer’s protocol; the limit of quantification was 3.1%, and the CVs ranged from 4.0% to 5.1%. All biomarker values used in the analysis were within the quantifiable detection ranges. Quality control procedures included internal controls and duplicate measurements for a subset of samples to ensure assay reliability. Χ 2 tests and t-tests were used to test the univariate associations between the dietary scores and baseline characteristics. P values for dietary indices were calculated using the Wilcoxon tests. Cox proportional hazards models were used to test the associations between the dietary scores and cancer incidence using a cohort study design following participants from age (in months) at enrollment through age (in months) at cancer diagnosis, death, or date through which state-specific cancer registry data are complete (varies by state 31 December, 2016–31 December, 2019). Associations between the dietary scores and cancer mortality were also assessed using age (in months) at death, or the latest date of complete vital status ascertainment (31 December, 2022) as the end of follow-up. Models were adjusted for sex (male, female), self-reported race or ethnicity (Black, White, other/unknown race), enrollment site (CHC or GP), education (less than high school, high school graduate, some college or technical training, and college graduate or higher education), annual household income (<$15,000, $15,000–$24,999, $25,000–$49,999, and $50,000 or more), marital status (married, not married), health insurance coverage (yes, no), BMI (<18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25–29.9, 30+), smoking status and intensity (current smoker of ≥20 cigarettes/d, current smoker of 10–19 cigarettes/d, current smoker of <10 cigarettes/d, former smoker of 20 or more cigarettes/d, former smoker of 10–19 cigarettes/d, former smoker of <10 cigarettes/d, and never smoker), history of diabetes (yes, no), heart attack (yes, no), stroke (yes, no), hypertension (yes, no), hypercholesterolemia (yes, no), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (yes, no) and HIV/AIDS (yes, no), total physical activity metabolic equivalent of task-hours per day (none, tertiles among those reporting activity), total hours spent sitting per day, total energy intake (kcal/d), and menopause status (postmenopausal, premenopausal) and having ever used hormone replacement therapy (yes, no) among females. The covariates were selected a priori based on existing literature or prior work in this cohort and their established associations with cancer risk, mortality, and diet. Additional analyses were conducted stratified by race (Black and White; there were too few in the other/unknown race/ethnicity category for separate analysis). Data were missing at random. The percent of missing values was <2% for any variables included in the analyses. Missing values were imputed using Proc MI in SAS version 9.4 with 5 imputations. All variables included in the models were used as predictors for the imputations. Although outcomes and dietary indices were used as predictors to impute adjustment covariates, we did not use the small number of imputed values for outcomes or for the dietary indices (our main covariates) in the main analyses. Each model for our main analyses included participants with nonmissing data for the outcome and dietary index and used imputed data for the adjustment variables leading to slightly different total sample sizes included in models for each outcome/dietary index combination.
Sensitivity analyses using participants without missing data for the covariates included in the models were performed producing very similar results. Likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without interaction terms were used to test for interactions between the dietary scores and race, and the range of P values across the multiple imputation models is presented. To assess potential nonlinear associations between dietary indices and cancer outcomes, we conducted restricted cubic spline analyses using 4 knots. Spearman correlation tests were performed to calculate the correlations between biomarkers and the dietary scores overall and by race. Statistical analyses were performed using the statistical software SAS Version 9.4. All statistical tests were 2-sided. A P value of ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses. We had over 90% statistical power to detect modest associations in all analyses.
Results
There was a moderate negative correlation between the DASH and EDIP scores ( r = −0.27, P < 0.0001), a strongly negative correlation between the DASH and EDIH scores ( r = −0.53, P < 0.0001), and a strongly positive correlation between EDIP and EDIH ( r = 0.65, P = 0.001) (data not shown in table). Across all 3 dietary patterns (DASH, EDIP, and EDIH), the higher diet quality groups had a greater proportion of females (62.8%–65.7%) than males ( Table 1 ). In the higher diet quality DASH group, 17.3% were college graduates or higher, compared with 9.0% in the lower diet quality DASH group. Similar trends were seen in EDIP (16.6% compared with 9.7%) and EDIH (16.3% compared with 9.9%). The proportion of never smokers was higher in high quality diet group measured by DASH (42.6%) compared with the lower quality diet group (29.7%). White participants compared with Black participants had better DASH and EDIP scores whereas EDIH scores did not differ by race ( Supplemental Table 1 ). Participants in the lowest diet quality quartiles, as assessed by DASH, EDIP, and EDIH, had a higher mortality rate compared with those in the highest diet quality quartiles ( Supplemental Table 2 ). There were very few associations between DASH, EDIP, and EDIH scores and cancer risk in minimally adjusted ( Supplemental Table 3 ) or fully adjusted models ( Table 2 , Supplemental Figure 2 ). Worsening EDIP scores were associated with a modest reduction in total cancer risk [hazard ratio (HR) per 1-SD increase: 0.98; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.96, 1.00]. An inverse association was also observed between EDIP and breast cancer risk, with participants in the lowest diet quality quartile showing a significantly lower risk than the highest quartile (HR = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.69, 0.97). Among Black participants, this association persisted, with the lowest EDIP quartile associated with reduced breast cancer risk (HR = 0.78; 95% CI: 0.64, 0.96). Among White participants, EDIP was associated with decreased total cancer risk in the second highest diet quality quartile (HR = 0.89; 95% CI: 0.81, 0.99). Lower prostate cancer risk among White participants was observed for both the second highest quality quartile (HR = 0.74; 95% CI: 0.55, 0.99) and the second lowest quality quartile (HR = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.49, 0.94) compared with the highest quality quartile for DASH. Among White participants, EDIH was associated with reduced prostate cancer risk in the second highest quality quartile (HR = 0.66; 95% CI: 0.47, 0.92) but was also associated with increased breast cancer risk in the second highest quality quartile (HR = 1.42; 95% CI: 1.09, 1.86) compared with the highest quality quartile. There was a significant interaction by race for prostate cancer risk only. Analyses of cancer mortality showed that a 1-SD decrease in DASH (worsening) among all participants indicated a modest increase in total cancer mortality (HR = 1.02; 95% CI: 1.00, 1.06), though quartile comparisons were not statistically significant ( Table 3 , Supplemental Figure 3 ). Among White participants, the lowest diet quality was associated with increased colorectal cancer mortality (HR = 2.30; 95% CI: 1.31, 4.05; P- trend = 0.01). For prostate cancer mortality among White participants, DASH was associated with increased risk of mortality in the lower (HR = 2.68; 95% CI: 1.00, 7.15) and lowest (HR = 2.88; 95% CI: 1.00, 8.30) diet quality quartiles. DASH also showed a significant interaction by race for colorectal cancer mortality ( P- interaction = 0.05–0.06). Among all participants, second highest diet quality as measured by EDIP was associated with lower total cancer mortality (HR = 0.87; 95% CI: 0.81, 0.94), and the 1-SD estimate also suggested a modest protective effect (HR = 0.98; 95% CI: 0.95, 1.00). For prostate cancer mortality, EDIP was associated with decreased risk in the higher quality quartile (HR = 0.67; 95% CI: 0.45, 0.99). Among White participants, we observed a decrease in total cancer mortality risk in the second highest EDIP quartile(HR = 0.83; 95% CI: 0.73, 0.95) and increased colorectal cancer mortality in the lower quality quartile (HR = 1.79; 95% CI: 1.07, 3.00). Among White participants, lower diet quality, as measured by EDIH, was associated with increased breast cancer mortality (HR = 1.97; 95% CI: 1.09, 3.56). In minimally adjusted models ( Supplemental Table 4 ), results were similar although may have been attenuated by full adjustment. Discussion
This study investigated the associations between 3 established dietary pattern scores and cancer incidence and mortality in a socioeconomically and racially diverse cohort in the Southern United States that is predominantly low income and Black. Generally, diet quality, as measured by DASH, EDIP, or EDIH scores was higher in females compared with males, and in White participants compared with Black participants. However, there was no clear pattern of association between dietary scores and overall risk of cancer incidence and mortality. A lower DASH score was linked to a significant increase in colorectal cancer mortality and, possibly, prostate cancer mortality, but only among White individuals for both associations. Similarly, our findings also reveal an inconsistent pattern regarding diet quality measured by the EDIP and EDIH score across the entire cohort. Specifically, better diet quality as measured by EDIH score seems to be associated with increased mortality, especially breast cancer. The significant interactions observed by race emphasize the importance of evaluating dietary patterns within diverse populations.
Some previous investigations into the impact of dietary patterns on cancer outcomes have found that better DASH scores are linked with a reduced risk of overall cancer mortality [ [36] , [37] , [38] , [39] ]. This correlation has been observed across diverse geographical settings, including United States, Canadian, and European cohorts [ [36] , [37] , [38] , [39] ]. It is noteworthy that although these cohorts predominantly consist of White populations, a similar association is evident in Black males [ 37 ]. Intriguingly, this association is not as pronounced in Black females [ 40 ]. These divergent outcomes highlight the existence of potential disparities in health outcomes related to diet within specific demographic groups.
Higher (or worse) EDIH and EDIP scores were designed to indicate inflammatory or insulinemic potential of diets. Some previous studies, particularly in predominantly White cohorts, have reported that higher EDIH scores are associated with an increased risk of prostate cancer and breast cancer, suggesting that diets with greater insulinemic potential may contribute to cancer risk and mortality [ 16 , [41] , [42] , [43] ]. However, there is some literature showing no association between dietary patterns measured by EDIH and EDIP and prostate cancer mortality [ 44 ]. Similarly, EDIP scores have been associated with an elevated risk of colorectal cancer in White males [ 45 ]. Interestingly, we observed an opposite-to-expected association between EDIH and breast cancer risk, where higher diet quality was associated with increased risk and mortality among White participants. This finding may reflect limitations in the EDIH construct’s ability to capture relevant dietary exposures or metabolic responses in certain subpopulations. Additionally, this association could be influenced by residual confounding, measurement error in dietary reporting, or unmeasured lifestyle factors that covary with diet quality. Given the stratified nature of our analysis and potential sample size constraints, the possibility of a chance finding cannot be ruled out. These results highlight the need for further research to validate dietary indices like EDIH in more diverse populations and to explore potential biological mechanisms underlying these associations. The significant interactions observed emphasize the importance of evaluating dietary patterns within diverse populations, as associations found in homogeneous cohorts may not be generalizable across demographic groups.
All 3 dietary indices performed comparably in this population, with significant correlations among them. The EDIH, EDIP, and the DASH score were developed primarily in cohorts with limited racial diversity, and their predictive validity may not generalize across racial groups or other socioeconomic groups. The observed discrepancy in cancer associations with EDIP and EDIH scores is also reflected in the lack of strong correlations with biomarkers in this study. Specifically, among White individuals, both EDIP and EDIH were weakly, although significantly, positively associated with hsCRP levels. In contrast, no such significant correlation was seen in Black individuals for EDIH, and the correlation with EDIP was comparatively much weaker. DASH scores were negatively correlated with hsCRP levels in White individuals and PGE-M levels in Black individuals, indicating both decreased inflammatory markers with better diet quality across both racial groups. This could indicate a somewhat better reflection of circulating marker levels for DASH in this population. However, the DASH diet positively correlated with C-peptide, HbA1c, and hsCRP levels in Black individuals and TNF-α in White individuals, pointing to an increase in inflammatory biomarkers despite a higher diet quality. Thus, it appears that the EDIP, EDIH, and DASH scores, as originally developed and although significantly correlated with each other in this population, may not fully reflect dietary potential and its influence on or possible mechanisms for health outcomes within this low-income and predominantly Black population.
Yet, even among White participants, our findings differ in several respects from prior literature. In addition to the possible differences in the relationship to biomarkers discussed above, the dietary assessment tool used in this study, a regionally adapted FFQ developed for the SCCS, differs from those used in other large cohorts. Thus, these patterns may reflect limitations in how well these indices capture culturally specific dietary practices, food access, or nutritional exposures relevant to cancer risk in Black and low-income populations.
Our study has some limitations. Although the DASH, EDIP, and EDIH scores were selected to capture distinct and biologically relevant aspects of diet quality, our findings suggest that these indices may not fully encompass the complexity of dietary behaviors in this diverse cohort. Additionally, the complexity of diet–cancer associations may not be fully captured by summary indices such as DASH, EDIP, and EDIH, which may overlook specific dietary components or behaviors more relevant to certain cancer types or subpopulations. Across most cancer outcomes, particularly in race-stratified analyses, associations were generally null or modest, and in some cases inconsistent with prior literature. Although the overall sample size is large, the relatively small number of White participants in the cohort may have limited the statistical power for stratified analyses by race and cancer type to detect modest associations. This limitation is reflected in the wide CIs observed for several associations, suggesting reduced statistical power to detect modest effects with precision for the smallest subgroup analyses. These findings highlight the importance of interpreting null or inconsistent results with caution, as some may be attributable to insufficient power rather than a true absence of association.
Despite using a modified FFQ to adjust for population-specific dietary patterns, potential biases associated with self-reported dietary information are inherent. Diet was assessed at a single time point before cancer diagnosis. If the participant’s diet changed after baseline and this change occurred during the etiologically relevant exposure window, this may be one possible explanation for our general lack of association with most outcomes and in most groups if a true association exists. Future studies should include dietary assessments at multiple time points for a more accurate estimation of long-term dietary exposure. Additionally, integrating dietary biomarkers including repeated measures may also complement self-reported dietary data and reduce misclassification errors associated with recall bias or reporting inaccuracies. The dietary scores, particularly EDIP and EDIH, were developed and validated in populations that have different characteristics compared with this population and using different FFQs, both of which may explain the lack of associations. However, there are also several additional strengths. SCCS participants include a majority of Black individuals and those with lower SES, groups underrepresented in other cohorts. Our study also adjusted for various confounding factors including low income that increase our study’s robustness as well as the ability to conduct stratified analyses.
In summary, our study highlights the need for more research on dietary patterns and cancer risk or mortality across diverse populations like SCCS. Future research should also consider the differences in dietary patterns and the consequential differences in biological mechanisms that may contribute to variations in dietary associations between populations.
