Modifiable risk factors for subsequent lethal prostate cancer among men with an initially negative prostate biopsy
Abstract
Background
Previously suggested modifiable risk factors for prostate cancer could have resulted from detection bias because diagnosis requires a biopsy. We investigated modifiable risk factors for a subsequent cancer diagnosis among men with an initially negative prostate biopsy.
Methods
In total, 10,396 participants of the Health Professionals Follow-up Study with an initial negative prostate biopsy after 1994 were followed for incident prostate cancer until 2017. Potential risk factors were based on previous studies in the general population. Outcomes included localised, advanced, and lethal prostate cancer.
Results
With 1851 prostate cancer cases (168 lethal) diagnosed over 23 years of follow-up, the 20-year risk of any prostate cancer diagnosis was 18.5% (95% CI: 17.7–19.3). Higher BMI and lower alcohol intake tended to be associated with lower rates of localised disease. Coffee, lycopene intake and statin use tended to be associated with lower rates of lethal prostate cancer. Results for other risk factors were less precise but compatible with and of similar direction as for men in the overall cohort.
Conclusions
Risk factors for future prostate cancer among men with a negative biopsy were generally consistent with those for the general population, supporting their validity given reduced detection bias, and could be actionable, if confirmed.
Subject terms: Risk factors, Prostate cancer, Lifestyle modification
Introduction
More than one million men undergo a prostate biopsy each year in the United States, a consequence of widespread prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening. Two-thirds of men with an elevated PSA can expect their biopsy results to be negative for cancer [ 1 , 2 ]. These negative biopsies are a form of harm from PSA screening, given the burden and risks of the biopsy itself [ 3 ], and men with a previous negative biopsy are more likely to not return for PSA screening than men without a prior negative biopsy [ 4 ].
At the same time, the burden of subsequent prostate cancer despite a negative biopsy is substantial. In the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, and Ovarian Cancer Screening (PLCO) trial, almost 30% of men with a negative prostate biopsy were ultimately diagnosed with prostate cancer with a median follow-up of 13 years [ 5 ]. Collectively, these data point to a need to identify risk factors for subsequent clinically significant prostate cancer among initially biopsy-negative men, ideally those that are actionable.
Several modifiable factors have been reported to be associated with the risk of development or progression of prostate cancer in the general population, which include smoking, obesity, physical activity, dietary lycopene intake, coffee intake, dairy, and other dietary factors [ 6 ]. However, it has been debated to what extent associations are driven by detection bias [ 7 ], i.e., confounding by common causes of potential risk factors and biopsy receipt. For example, obesity was found to be inversely associated with nonadvanced prostate cancer, but obese men were less likely to have a prostate biopsy [ 7 ]. One approach to address detection bias is the restriction of a study population to men who all had a prior biopsy. Another clinically important question is which risk factors are modifiable for individuals with a prior negative prostate biopsy who may continue to worry about their future prostate cancer risk.
This study aimed to address both questions and assess 16 potential risk factors for prostate cancer among men with an initial negative prostate biopsy in a large prospective cohort in the U.S. We examined to what extent the long-term risk of advanced and lethal prostate cancer after an initial negative prostate biopsy could potentially be affected by modifiable risk factors. In addition, we quantified the risk of overall and lethal prostate cancer among individuals in whom the prostate biopsy was initially negative.
Methods
Study population
The Health Professionals Follow-up Study (HPFS) enrolled 51,529 US men working in health professions aged 40–75 years in 1986 [ 8 , 9 ]. Participants completed a baseline questionnaire at study enrollment, and have been followed by mailed and web-based questionnaires every 2 years to update exposure information and disease outcomes, with >90% follow-up per cycle.
Starting in 1994, participants were asked if they ever had a prostate biopsy or transrectal ultrasound for prostate examination, and this information was updated for each 2-year interval on subsequent follow-up biennial questionnaires (except 1996; the 1998 questionnaire asked about 1994–98). This study included men who reported a prostate biopsy or transrectal ultrasound examination on 1994–2010 questionnaires and did not have a prostate cancer diagnosis within the next 12 months. We set 2010 as the end of the inclusion period to have sufficient follow-up for cancer incidence.
The biopsy date was imputed as 12 months before the return date of the questionnaire on which a participant reported a prostate biopsy or transrectal ultrasound. The biopsy was defined as negative if participants did not report a diagnosis of prostate cancer during the ensuing 12 months.
Assessment of risk factors
We examined 16 prostate cancer risk factors for which at least suggestive data supported a potential role in the development or progression of prostate cancer or advanced prostate cancer [ 10 – 23 ]. All exposures, confounders, and effect modifiers were collected before the questionnaire that reported the prostate biopsy or ultrasound. Repeatedly measured exposures, such as diet and physical activity, were included as the cumulative average value between cohort enrolment in 1986 and study baseline, which is the date of the initially negative biopsy. Medication use and medical conditions were defined as any report of use on questionnaires preceding start of follow-up. Information on weight at age 21 was collected in 1986; waist circumference was assessed in 1987, 1996 and 2008; weight, aspirin use, smoking status, and physical activity were collected in 1986 and on each biennial questionnaire; cholesterol-lowering medication use was collected in 1990 and on each subsequent biennial questionnaire (from 2000 on specifically statins), and consistent with prior studies we presented all as statins in this study [ 20 ]. Dietary and nutrient intakes were assessed by a validated food frequency questionnaire [ 24 ] at baseline in 1986 and every 4 years thereafter, which served to estimate total energy intake for energy adjustment and included intakes of tomato sauce, coffee, dairy products, calcium, processed meat, red meat, fish, and alcohol. For physical activity, using a validated questionnaire administered every 2 years, weekly energy expenditure was estimated by multiplying the typical intensity expressed in metabolic equivalent of task (MET) (the ratio of metabolic rate during the activity to metabolic rate at rest) [ 25 , 26 ] by the reported hours spent per week. Activities with MET > 6 were categorised as vigorous activity, including jogging, running, bicycling, lap swimming, tennis, squash/racquetball, calisthenics/rowing, and stair climbing [ 11 ]. Dietary lycopene intake was based on the consumption of food items including tomatoes, tomato sauce, tomato juice, pizza, salsa, picante or taco sauce, ketchup or red chili sauce, watermelon and pink grapefruit [ 12 ].
We used subject matter-based exposure categorisations consistent with prior studies for coffee intake, BMI [ 27 ], waist circumference [ 27 ], smoking [ 9 ], alcohol [ 28 ], statin and aspirin use [ 20 , 29 ], and intakes of tomato sauce [ 30 ], calcium [ 31 ], fish [ 32 ] and processed and red meat [ 33 ]. Vigorous physical activity as well as dietary intake of lycopene and dairy were categorised as quintiles within each questionnaire cycle [ 11 , 30 , 34 , 35 ]. For BMI at age 21 and waist circumference, for which we used the only or the first assessment, a missingness indicator was used in categorisations. Other risk factors were carried forward from prior questionnaires, as most exposures of interest typically have limited changes over short time (given the 2-year questionnaire frequency), likely resulting in limited misclassification. Participants with missing risk factor data after this imputation were not included.
Assessment of confounders and effect modifiers
Data on non-modifiable prostate cancer risk factors, age, race, and height, were collected in 1986; family history of prostate cancer in father or brother was derived from the 1990, 1992 and 1996 questionnaires. Personal history of diabetes was collected in 1986 and on each biennial questionnaire. Starting in 1994, participants reported on biennial questionnaires whether they had undergone a PSA test and whether the PSA was elevated in the preceding 2 years. We defined the PSA testing intensity as reporting a PSA test in half or more questionnaire cycles since 1994 until the initial negative biopsy.
Ascertainment of prostate cancer cases
Incident prostate cancers were first reported by the participating health professionals on biennial questionnaires, subsequently confirmed through medical records and pathology reports [ 36 ], and followed further through prostate cancer follow-up questionnaires, including for the development of metastases. Deaths were ascertained through family members and the National Death Index, with the cause of death adjudication by study physicians based on all available information including medical records. We classified prostate cancer as localised (T1 or T2 and N0, M0), advanced (T3b, T4, N1, or M1), or lethal (distant metastases at diagnosis or during follow-up, prostate cancer-specific death). The stage used in classifications was pathologic (from prostatectomy) if available and clinical if the pathologic stage was not available.
Statistical analysis
Participants who reported a prostate cancer biopsy or ultrasound on a biennial questionnaire and did not have a prostate cancer diagnosis within 1 year following the biopsy date entered the biopsy-negative group; the first participant entered this group in January 1994, and the last participant in December 2010. The start of follow-up was the return date of the questionnaire on which participants indicated that they had had a prostate biopsy, assuming that the biopsy occurred at the mid-point of the 2-year questionnaire interval. These participants were then followed for a diagnosis of any prostate cancer, death, or end of follow-up for this study (January 2017), whichever occurred first. Cumulative incidence was estimated using the Fine-Gray model, with adjustment for calendar year at the initial negative biopsy.
We set the date of initial negative biopsy detection as the baseline in this study. Exposures and confounders were collected before and most near that date. Incident prostate cancer was the event, and men were censored at the end of the follow-up (January 2017) or last questionnaire date. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), and the time scale was follow-up years since the date of meeting entry criteria for a negative biopsy. Calendar year at biopsy was used as a stratification variable, controlling for potential secular trends by comparing among men who had their negative biopsy in the same calendar year. The distribution of calendar years of initial negative biopsy is provided as Supplementary Table 1 .
We adjusted all models for age at initial negative biopsy (continuous), height (continuous), family history of prostate cancer (yes, no), PSA testing in ≥50% of possible time periods (yes, no), PSA value at biopsy (normal, elevated, missing/unknown), race (White, African-American, Asian-American, other), history of diabetes (yes, no), and BMI (< 25, 25–29.9, ≥30 kg/m 2 ). Selected models incorporated additional potential confounders (see table footnotes), as appropriate. For instance, all dietary intakes were additionally adjusted for total energy intake (quintiles). Trends (slopes) were estimated by treating exposures as continuous variables, except lycopene which was modelled using category medians as the linear predictor [ 12 , 30 ].
In sensitivity analyses, to distinguish tumours missed on the initial biopsy from those that truly progressed to detectable tumours, we started follow-up 3 years after the initial negative biopsy, thereby excluding tumours diagnosed shortly after a negative biopsy. We also assessed multiplicative effect measure modification by PSA level, reported as “normal” or “elevated,” before the negative biopsy. For comparability with other studies, we also report the outcome of aggressive prostate cancer as defined by the Prostate Cancer Cohort Consortium (T4 or N1 or M1 or Gleason ≥8) [ 37 ] (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3 ). We further excluded participants whose BMI was lower than 20 kg/m 2 , and re-defined alcohol consumption status as never, former, and current drinkers as sensitivity analysis. Finally, we treated non-prostate cancer death as a competing event, using the Fine-Gray model (Supplementary Table 4 ).
Analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4M7 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).
Results
Study population and absolute risks
Among the 51,529 men in HPFS, 16,492 participants reported at least one prostate biopsy from 1994 to 2010, including 12,445 men who reported a negative prostate biopsy. Our study population included participants who were alive and cancer-free (excluding non-melanoma skin cancer) at the start of follow-up in 1994 (1545 participants had cancer and/or died before this date). We excluded men with unknown date of birth ( n = 1) or missing exposure or confounder data after carry-forward imputation ( n = 503). As a result, 10,396 men had a prostate biopsy with negative results were included in the current study. Of these, 45% of men had further subsequent negative biopsies.
During 127,835 person-years of follow-up, we documented 1851 cases of prostate cancer after an initial negative biopsy, of which 1387 were diagnosed ≥3 years after the initial negative biopsy. The incident cases were categorised as 1370 localised prostate cancers (1008 cases after ≥3 years), 118 advanced prostate cancers (85 cases after ≥3 years) and 168 lethal prostate cancers (127 cases after ≥3 years).
Men who had a negative biopsy were on average older, reported slightly less alcohol consumption, received more PSA tests, and were more likely to have an elevated PSA level than men without a negative biopsy (Table 1 ).

Adiposity and physical activity
Higher current BMI was suggestively inversely associated with rates of overall (HR per 5 kg/m 2 : 0.95; 95% CI: 0.88–1.02) and localised prostate cancer (HR per 5 kg/m 2 : 0.93; 95% CI: 0.86–1.01; Table 2 ). Estimates were of similar magnitude in the sensitivity analyses that considered only cancers diagnosed more than 3 years after the initial biopsy (Table 3 ), when stratifying by PSA level before diagnosis (Table 4 ), when excluding participants with BMI below 20 kg/m 2 (Supplementary Table 5 ), and when considering BMI at age 21 years as a measure of adiposity earlier in life (Table 2 ). Results were inconclusive for waist circumference, vigorous physical activity, and the outcomes of advanced and lethal prostate cancer (Tables 2 – 4 ).

Smoking and alcohol
Results for smoking status were inconclusive for all outcomes given the low prevalence of smoking in this population. Alcohol intake was associated with a higher rate of overall prostate cancer (HR per 5 g/day: 1.04; 95% CI: 1.02–1.06), driven by elevated rates of localised disease (HR per 5 g/day: 1.05; 95% CI: 1.03–1.07). No associations with advanced or lethal disease were observed (Table 2 ). The association between alcohol intake and localised prostate cancer was similar when only considering cancers diagnosed >3 years after the initial biopsy (Table 3 ) but appeared confined to men with elevated PSA levels before biopsy (Table 4 ). When comparing never, former, and current alcohol drinkers, current drinkers tended to have slightly higher rates of localised disease and lower rates of lethal disease than never-drinkers, but the results were less precise (Supplementary Table 6 ).
Dietary lycopene and tomato products
Associations between dietary lycopene intake and its main source, tomato products, were inconclusive for total, localised, and advanced prostate cancer (Table 2 ). Participants with modest or high lycopene intakes had lower rates of lethal prostate cancer than those with the lowest intakes (HR per 3000 µg/day: 0.83; 95% CI: 0.70–0.97), an observation consistent in sensitivity analyses (Tables 3 and 4 ).
Coffee intake
Coffee intake was not associated with total or localised prostate cancer (Tables 2 – 4 ). In contrast, coffee intake was inversely associated with lethal prostate cancer, with a dose-response (HR per 1 cup/day: 0.89; 95% CI: 0.78–1.00). Results were similar for advanced disease (Table 2 ). Compatible associations were observed in the sensitivity analyses (Tables 3 and 4 ).
Dairy products and calcium intake
Results for calcium intake and the major dietary source of calcium, dairy products, were inconclusive for all outcomes (Tables 2 – 4 ).
Meat and fish intake
For processed meat intake, the results were not conclusive. Higher intakes tended to be associated with higher rates of advanced prostate cancer with a suggestively linear dose-response relation (HR per 1 serving/week: 1.06; 95% CI: 0.97–1.15; Table 2 ), similarly for lethal prostate cancer (HR per 1 serving/week: 1.04; 95% CI: 0.97–1.12), but both estimates were imprecise.
Red meat intake was not linearly associated with rates of advanced or lethal disease, but as compared to men who had <3 servings/week, all higher categories of intake tended to be associated with slightly higher risk (Table 2 ).
For fish consumption, data were inconclusive regarding a potentially inverse linear association with localised prostate cancer (Tables 2 and 3 ).
Commonly used medications
Statin use tended to be associated with lower rates of lethal prostate cancer (HR 0.62, 95% CI: 0.38–1.00), but not with overall (HR 1.07, 95% CI: 0.96–1.21) or localised disease (HR 1.12, 95% CI: 0.98–1.28). Aspirin use was not clearly associated with any of the prostate cancer outcomes, although the data were slightly more compatible with an association with lower rates of lethal disease (HR 0.83, 95% CI: 0.59–1.17).
Discussion
Modifiable risk factors for prostate cancer have typically been assessed in populations of men who were presumed to be initially cancer-free. With the substantial burden of undiagnosed prostate cancer [ 38 ], it was not fully clear to what extent the observed associations between potentially modifiable risk factors and a prostate cancer diagnosis were driven by biased detection of preexisting disease. This study analysed men with an initial negative prostate biopsy, which may reduce potential detection bias. The results from the present study should not be considered in isolation, but rather to indicate that associations between modifiable risk factors and prostate cancer outcomes among men with a prior negative biopsy were largely compatible with associations in the general population (Table 5 ).

We found a substantial risk of prostate cancer following negative prostate biopsy. The 20-year risk of lethal prostate cancer after a negative prostate biopsy was 1.5%. Risk of prostate cancer despite initial negative biopsy reported in western countries [ 39 – 43 ] are largely similar to our data. The PLCO study reported 20-year risk as 26.4% (95% CI: 24.8–28.1) for prostate cancer incidence and 1.2% (95% CI: 0.9–1.7) for mortality among men with elevated PSA or digital rectal exams followed within one year by a negative biopsy [ 39 ]. These absolute risks are presumably specific to men with an indication for a biopsy. Of note, many biopsies in these studies and in our study that were deemed negative were performed decades ago without guidance of magnetic resonance imaging and fewer sampled cores. They would be considered inadequate by today’s standards. This inherent feature of a long-term study does influence absolute risks of subsequent prostate cancer despite a negative biopsy, making them higher than achievable by current clinical practice. However, undersampling was likely non-differential with respect to modifiable risk factors, and in our study, all comparisons were between biopsies performed in the same calendar year. Undersampling is thus unlikely to systematically influence estimates for any of the risk factors. In addition, risk factor associations, at least for total prostate cancer, among men with negative biopsies in earlier calendar years and those in later calendar years were statistically compatible with each other (Supplementary Table 7 ).
Our findings about modifiable risk factors from the current study should be considered in conjunction with the substantial evidence about these factors that has accumulated from HPFS [ 9 , 11 , 12 , 20 , 27 , 28 , 30 – 32 , 34 – 36 , 44 – 48 ] (Table 5 ) and many other observational studies [ 10 , 13 – 19 , 21 – 23 ]. For many of the factors considered, although results from the current study were imprecise, they were largely compatible with associations in the general population. Associations reported in the general population include physical activity as a potential protective factor against lethal prostate cancer [ 11 ], a potential inverse association between high lycopene intake and risk of lethal prostate cancer [ 47 ], and potential positive associations between high processed and red meat consumption with the risk of lethal prostate cancer [ 35 , 44 ].
Our results on risk of localised prostate cancer may be informative for understanding disease aetiology. As in a pooled study of 830,772 men worldwide [ 10 ] and in HPFS in general [ 27 ], we found an inverse association between high BMI and localised prostate cancer, suggesting that detection bias does not fully explain this association. However, the association may still be non-causal due to differences in PSA [ 49 ] and prostate volume [ 50 ] with obesity, potentially influencing biopsy sensitivity [ 51 ]. For alcohol intake, findings regarding its relation to prostate cancer differ by alcohol type and outcome definition [ 22 , 23 , 28 ]. A meta-analysis of cohort studies showed a linear association of liquor intake with non-aggressive prostate cancer [ 22 ], similar to our findings of alcohol intake and higher rates of localised disease after an initial negative biopsy. It is possible that the investigated risk factors have already been acting for years before the negative prostate biopsy, and thus it will require further study with data on changes in risk factors to what extent interventions after a negative biopsy may be effective.
Our study has several strengths, including the prospective cohort design of a sample of men with excellent access to and understanding of health care, long-term follow-up, comprehensive profiling of prostate cancer risk factors, including PSA screening, and detailed and repeated data collection, as well as confirmation of prostate cancer diagnosis and death with detailed recording. There are several potential limitations in our study. First, biopsy-negative participants were identified by self-report, which may lead to the misclassification of the target population. However, the cohort is comprised of health professionals, which makes the self-reported information more reliable than in the general population. Second, the exact date of biopsy examination was not collected. When excluding prostate cancer cases diagnosed within 3 years after the assumed negative biopsy date in sensitivity analyses, most results were similar compared to the main analysis that used a presumed 1-year interval. Third, a subsequent diagnosis of prostate cancer requires another prostate biopsy, and risk factors may in part be associated with such another biopsy. For example, a population-based study from Australia found high alcohol consumption was associated with increased risk of a positive biopsy, with fewer biopsies performed among heavy alcohol users [ 52 ]. The current study was limited by not having access to the timing and indication of the diagnostic biopsy. Other limitations include the crude classification of PSA values as “normal” and “elevated,” as reported by the participants, which did not allow further evaluating the indication for biopsy, and the moderate sample size, leading to the wide confidence intervals in subgroup results and for less common exposures and those with limited variance in our population. Given that men with an initial negative biopsy have a substantial risk of subsequent lethal prostate cancer, even modest associations may be of public health interest. However, such modest associations will require further confirmation in additional studies, given that they may still be the result of residual confounding. Of note, for power reasons, the exposures that we studied were prevalent exposures before the negative biopsy, not incident exposures after the biopsy. We thus cannot distinguish whether changing a risk factor after a prostate biopsy would potentially confer similar benefits as potentially longer-term exposure before the biopsy.
In conclusion, risk factors for prostate cancer among men with an initially negative prostate biopsy were compatible with data on these potential risk factors for prostate cancer in the general population. If such results were confirmed in further studies, including randomised controlled trials where feasible, then men who were not found to have prostate cancer on a prostate biopsy would have additional means to reduce their remaining future risk of prostate cancer. In addition, our results suggest that associations of these risk factors with prostate cancer risk have not mainly been driven by differential access to prostate biopsies. Results need to be validated with larger sample sizes that would ideally also allow for assessing changes in risk factors after a negative prostate biopsy.
