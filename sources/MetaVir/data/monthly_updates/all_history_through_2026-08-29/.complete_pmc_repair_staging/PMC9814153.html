Abstract

Background

Although adolescent diet has been proposed to contribute to prostate cancer (PCa) development, no studies have investigated the relation between adolescent dietary patterns and PCa risk or mortality.

Methods

Using data from 164,079 men in the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study, we performed factor analysis to identify dietary patterns at ages 12–13 years and then used Cox proportional hazards regression to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of total (n = 17,861), non-advanced (n = 15,499), advanced (n = 2362), and fatal PCa (n = 832).

Results

Although not entirely consistent across analyses, a higher adolescent plant-based pattern (characterised by vegetables, fruits, and dark bread) score was associated with slightly reduced risks of total (fully adjusted HRQ5vs.Q1 = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.89–0.98, p trend=0.003) and non-advanced PCa (HR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.87–0.96, p trend<0.001), whereas no associations were observed for advanced or fatal PCa, or for Western modern (characterised by sweets, processed meat, beef, cheese, and pizza) or Western traditional (characterised gravy, eggs, potatoes and white bread) patterns.

Conclusion

We found evidence to support a modest, protective role for a plant-based dietary pattern during adolescence on PCa risk. If confirmed in future studies, our findings may help to inform the development of new, primary prevention strategies for PCa.

Subject terms: Risk factors, Cancer epidemiology

Background

To date, most epidemiologic research on prostate cancer (PCa) has focused on mid- to later-life exposures, such as adult diet. However, an emerging body of literature supports an earlier-life onset to prostate carcinogenesis, such as during periods of rapid development (e.g., adolescence) when the prostate may be more susceptible to carcinogenic exposures. Support for this hypothesis includes the observation of small prostate tumours and precursor lesions in young men in their 20–30s [1–3], the emergence of race as a risk factor for PCa incidence and mortality in middle-aged men in their 40–50s [4], and the frequent observation of positive associations between younger age at migration from low- to high-PCa risk countries (e.g., from Japan to the US [5]) and PCa risk. This last observation suggests that early-life environmental factors, such as adolescent diet, may contribute to PCa development, as has been observed for other types of cancers, including breast, colorectum, thyroid, and pancreatic cancer [6–11].

In our previous NIH-AARP Diet and Health (AARP) Study analyses, we also observed suggestive findings for intake of various, individual foods and nutrients during adolescence (milk, cheese, ice cream, calcium, unprocessed beef, tomatoes, and broccoli) with PCa risk and mortality [12–14]. However, the direction of our findings was occasionally unexpected (e.g., inverse association for adolescent unprocessed beef intake) and some findings attenuated with adjustment for intake of other foods and nutrients (e.g., positive findings for adolescent milk, dairy products, and calcium with PCa risk that attenuated to null after adjustment for other foods and nutrients, particularly red meat). These findings suggest that dietary pattern analysis may be an effective framework for studying adolescent diet and PCa because it assesses the overall quality of diet and the inter-relationships between consumption of individual foods, rather than focusing on single foods or nutrients [15]. To our knowledge, no previous studies have investigated adolescent dietary patterns in relation to PCa risk or mortality. Therefore, we extended our previous analyses to investigate the relation between adolescent dietary patterns and PCa risk and mortality in the AARP Study.

Methods

The AARP Study is a large prospective cohort study administered by the US National Cancer Institute. In 1995, baseline questionnaires were mailed to AARP members 50–70 years of age residing in six states (California, Florida, Louisiana, New Jersey, North Carolina, and Pennsylvania) and two metropolitan areas (Atlanta and Detroit). Approximately six months later, consenting participants completed a supplemental risk factor questionnaire including information on diet at ages 12-13 years. Participants have since been followed for cancer by linkage to 11 state cancer registries and for death by linkage to the US Social Security Administration Death Master File, National Death Index, and questionnaire responses from next of kin. For the present analysis, male participants were required to have completed the supplemental risk factor questionnaire (n = 213,090) and were excluded if they: (1) completed any questionnaires by proxy (n = 19,184); (2) reported any cancers including PCa before the date of completion of the risk factor questionnaire (n = 15,703); (3) reported extreme energy intake during adolescence (<500 or >4500 kcal/day, n = 10,003 [16]); or (4) had more than four missing adolescent food items (n = 4121). The final analytic cohort included 164,079 men. The study was approved by the National Cancer Institute’s Special Studies Institutional Review Board.

Adolescent diet was assessed by a 37-item food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) included on the supplemental risk factor questionnaire. For each food item, participants were asked to select their frequency of intake using eight pre-defined frequency categories from never to 2 or more times per day. Portion size was estimated using data from the Department of Agriculture 1965–1966 Household Food Consumption Survey for boys 12–13 years of age [17]. Dietary patterns were identified by factor analysis using the PROC FACTOR procedure in SAS version 9.4. We used Velicer’s minimum average partial test to determine the number of factors to retain [18]. The three identified factors (Supplemental Table 1) were rotated by varimax rotation, energy-adjusted by the residual method [19], and categorised into quintiles, similar to previous dietary pattern analyses [20].

PCa data were obtained from state cancer registries. We defined total PCa as a first primary diagnosis of PCa (ICD-9 185 or ICD-10 C61); advanced PCa as clinical stages T3–T4, lymph node involvement (N1), metastasis (M1), or PCa death (PCa listed as the underlying cause of death); and fatal PCa as PCa death. We used Cox proportional hazards regression to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of PCa incidence and mortality. Time-at-risk was calculated from the month of return of the risk factor questionnaire to the month of PCa diagnosis (PCa risk analyses only), loss to follow-up, death, or December 31, 2011, whichever came first.

We performed adjustment in a sequential fashion and evaluated the influence of individual and sets of covariates on the point estimates for each dietary pattern. Our first set of models included only age, and our second set included a larger number of covariates that could serve only as potential confounders or could address detection bias (age; race; family history of PCa; education; marital status; smoking history; adult alcohol intake; adult body mass index [BMI]; adult waist circumference; adolescent and adult physical activity; history of prostate-specific antigen [PSA] tests and digital rectal examinations [DREs], diabetes; and father’s occupation). In our third set of models, we added variables that could potentially serve as either confounders or mediators (adolescent BMI and attained height), and in our fourth set, we added adult intake of individual food groups to investigate the independent effect of adolescent diet. As none of these covariates (individually or combined with other covariates) explained changes across models, we present only age- and fully adjusted results.

Finally, we performed sensitivity analyses: (1) restricted to younger men (<55 years) at the time of completion of the adolescent FFQ because of their likely greater accuracy of adolescent diet recall [21, 22]; (2) stratified by the number of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) tests in the 3 years before baseline to further investigate the potential for detection bias; and (3) stratified by calendar time to capture food availability during early adolescence (12–13 years) and the likelihood of consistent dietary intake into later adolescence when the prostate grows and develops rapidly (passed through puberty completely before the Great Depression or World War II [WWII], partially during WWII, and completely following WWII).

Results

Of the 164,079 eligible participants, 17,861 were diagnosed with PCa (15,499 non-advanced and 2362 advanced) and 832 men died from PCa over 1,878,681 person-years of follow-up. The average age of participants was 62 years when they enrolled and 74 at the end of follow up. Factor analysis identified three dietary patterns consumed during adolescence: (1) a Western modern pattern characterised by greater intake of sweets, processed meat, ground and roast beef, cheese, pizza, French fries, tuna, mayonnaise, and peanut butter; (2) a plant-based pattern characterised by greater intake of vegetables, fruits, dark bread, and tuna; and (3) a Western traditional pattern characterised by greater intake of meat (gravy, bacon, and chicken), eggs, potatoes, other vegetables (e.g., corn, peas, green beans, and legumes), white bread, butter, and whole milk (Supplemental Table 1). Participants with higher scores for each of the adolescent dietary patterns were less likely to be non-Hispanic White, but differed on most other characteristics. Men with a high Western modern pattern score were more likely to have received a college education or higher; be unmarried; have fathers with professional, managerial, and other non-manual occupations; have a higher BMI in adolescence; have engaged in physical activity in both adolescence and adulthood; be never smokers; and have been screened for PCa more than once at baseline. In contrast, men with high adolescent plant-based and traditional pattern scores were less likely to have received a college education or higher and to have a father with a non-manual occupation, and more likely to have passed through puberty after WWII (as well as before WWII for the plant-based pattern), to have ever smoked, and to have been screened for PCa no more than once by baseline. Men with high plant-based pattern scores also had a lower BMI in adolescence and were more likely to engage in physical activity in adolescence and adulthood, whereas the opposite pattern was observed for men with high Western traditional diet scores. Men with high traditional scores tended to have higher BMI and to engage in less physical activity throughout life and to have diabetes as adults (Table 1).

As adult diet could potentially confound associations between adolescent dietary patterns and PCa, we next compared adult diet intake across adolescent dietary patterns. Men with high adolescent Western modern pattern scores were more likely to consume fruits, dark green vegetables, and tomatoes, and less likely to consume red meat as adults than those with lower scores. In contrast, men with high adolescent plant-based pattern scores consumed less fruit and whole grains, more red meat, and a similar amount of vegetables as adults than men with lower scores. Finally, men with high adolescent Western traditional pattern scores maintained some elements of a traditional diet (less fruit and more meat intake), but changed to consume fewer dairy products as adults (Table 1).

In analyses investigating adolescent dietary patterns in relation to PCa risk and mortality, a higher adolescent plant-based pattern score was associated with slightly reduced risks of total PCa (fully-adjusted HR comparing Q5 to Q1 = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.89–0.98, p trend=0.003) and non-advanced PCa (HR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.87–0.96, p trend<0.001; Table 2). Trends were not linear, however, with most of the reduced risk observed in the highest quintile. Men in the highest quintile consumed fruit and vegetables a mean of 15 times/week (in energy-adjusted analyses) compared to 11 times/week in men in the lowest quintile. No trends were observed for higher plant-based dietary pattern scores with advanced or fatal PCa. No significant trends were also observed for adolescent Western modern and traditional pattern scores with risks of total, non-advanced, advanced, or fatal PCa.

Discussion

In this large prospective analysis of adolescent dietary patterns and PCa risk, higher adolescent plant-based dietary pattern scores were associated with slightly reduced risks of total and non-advanced PCa, but not with advanced or fatal PCa. These associations were observed overall and in screened men. No associations were observed for Western modern and traditional patterns with PCa risk or mortality.

To our knowledge, our study is the first to investigate the relation between adolescent dietary patterns and PCa risk or mortality. The only other similar study is an older cohort study that examined an adolescent vegetarian lifestyle and having Seventh-day Adventist parents in relation to PCa risk [23]. This study observed no association for practicing a vegetarian lifestyle at home before age 15 and having at least one Adventist parent with PCa risk (HR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.51–1.26), although the magnitude of its association was similar to our inverse association (HR = 0.91). Our current findings for a plant-based dietary pattern during adolescence are also consistent with some of those from our previous analysis of individual plant products in the AARP Study, in which we observed inverse associations for adolescent tomato and non-starch vegetable intake with risks of total and/or non-advanced PCa, and null associations with risks of advanced and fatal PCa [14]. They differ, however, from our previous findings for adolescent intake of other plant products, including: (1) fruit juice, total fruit, and dark bread, which were associated with increased risks of total and/or non-advanced PCa; (2) legumes, fibre, and vegetable protein, which were associated with increased risks of advanced and/or fatal PCa; and (3) broccoli and fruit juice, which were associated with decreased risks of advanced and/or fatal PCa. As many of these plant products loaded onto the plant-based dietary pattern, reasons for differences between findings are not entirely clear, but could possibly be explained by intake of plant products unrelated to the plant-based dietary pattern. This may also explain differences in findings for Western modern and Western traditional dietary patterns with PCa risk and mortality than for some of the individual foods that loaded onto these patterns (e.g., ice cream, cheese, and unprocessed beef [12, 13]).

With respect to a plant-based dietary pattern, several possible mechanisms could potentially explain our modest, inverse findings, including a true protective effect of a diet rich in fruits and vegetables during adolescence, as well as non-causal explanations. Plant product intake could potentially protect against PCa risk by reducing antioxidant cell damage, increasing sex hormone binding globulin levels, and decreasing insulin resistance [24–27]. Each of these potential mechanisms could be even more important during adolescence when the developing prostate gland may be more susceptible to carcinogenic exposures. With respect to our conflicting findings for total/non-advanced and advanced/fatal PCa, these may possibly be explained by a greater role for plant product intake in the early than later stages of PCa development, as was observed for adult tomato intake with PCa risk and mortality in the Health Professionals Follow-up Study. In that study, inverse associations for tomato intake were stronger for or limited to low- than high-grade PCa and total than fatal PCa [28], similar to our inverse associations for a plant-based dietary pattern with total and non-advanced PCa but not with advanced or fatal PCa.

In addition to a true protective effect of a plant-based dietary pattern, possible non-causal explanations for our inverse findings include detection bias, residual confounding, and chance. Men with higher adolescent plant-based dietary pattern scores were slightly less likely to have been screened for PCa at baseline, which could have potentially contributed to an inverse association with non-advanced PCa, as these tumours are typically detected by screening. However, the fact that our inverse findings were limited to screened men at baseline makes this explanation less likely, although we cannot rule out the possibility of residual detection bias by subsequent differences in PSA screening frequency by dietary pattern score after baseline. Our findings may also possibly be explained by residual confounding by correlates of a plant-based dietary pattern during adolescence. We adjusted for several adolescent (e.g., father’s occupation as a marker of adolescent socioeconomic status [SES]) and adult covariates with minimal influence on our point estimates, but the potential for confounding by other unaddressed confounders remains. In addition, although our observed p-trends for adolescent plant-based pattern scores were significant, inverse associations were modest and limited to the top quintile. This could suggest that only a very high plant-based diet during adolescence is protective against PCa or that only the top category had sufficient correct exposure classification to detect a difference in risk. Finally, chance is also a possible explanation for our findings.

With respect to adolescent dietary patterns, our finding of three main adolescent dietary patterns is similar to that from the only other study, to our knowledge, to have presented these type of data, the Nurses’ Health Study II (NHSII). This study identified three main adolescent dietary patterns, including prudent, Western, and fast food patterns [20]. They also found that dietary patterns in adolescence and adulthood were moderately correlated [10, 20], which differs from our findings. However, we expected to observe a greater change in diet over time because AARP participants were older (means age = 61.8 vs. mid-40s in NHSII) and many of them passed through puberty during major world events that affected food availability in the US (i.e., the Great Depression and WWII) rather than in the 1970s–1980s. As a result, correlates of dietary patterns likely also changed over time. For example, earlier last century, a Western modern dietary pattern may have been more likely to indicate a higher SES (i.e., the ability to dine out), whereas a plant-based dietary pattern may have been a marker of lower SES (i.e., the inability to afford meat). In contrast, the opposite correlations are observed today, which may explain some of the shift from an adolescent Western modern to a more plant-based diet in adulthood and from an adolescent plant-based diet to a more Western modern or traditional diet in adulthood.

Strengths of our study include: (1) its prospective design (i.e., pre-diagnostic assessment of adolescent diet) to establish a temporal relation between adolescent diet and PCa; (2) large sample size to enable us to evaluate multiple PCa outcomes and to detect the associations of smaller magnitude; and (3) detailed data collection to control for numerous factors related to adolescent diet and PCa. Our study also has some limitations. First, although adolescent diet was assessed before PCa diagnosis, it was still assessed, on average, 50 years after adolescence, which likely contributed to some degree of measurement error. This may potentially explain our null results for Western modern and traditional dietary pattern scores and our modest results for a plant-based dietary pattern score. A second limitation of the AARP Study is its use of a short adolescent FFQ (i.e., 37 rather than >100 food items), which may have also limited our ability to detect associations. However, foods included on the AARP FFQ were selected carefully to represent many of the foods consumed earlier last century, possibly reducing this concern. In addition, the three adolescent dietary patterns identified in our study were very similar to those identified in the NHSII using a 124-item FFQ [20]. Finally, PCa screening information was collected only before baseline, making it difficult to rule out detection bias as an explanation for our findings.

In conclusion, although not entirely consistent across analyses, we found some evidence to support a modest, protective role for a plant-based dietary pattern during adolescence on PCa risk. If confirmed in future studies, these findings could help to inform the development of new, primary prevention strategies for PCa.
