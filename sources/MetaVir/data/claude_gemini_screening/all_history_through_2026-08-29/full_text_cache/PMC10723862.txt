Cancer Risk Factors in Southern Brazil: Report of a Comprehensive, Matched Case-Control Study
PURPOSE
To evaluate cancer risk factors among cancer cases and controls from Southern Brazil, to analyze a multigene hereditary panel testing (MGPT, 26 genes) for breast cancer (BC) and colorectal cancer (CCR) cases diagnosed age younger than 50 years and to characterize them for hereditary cancer syndrome (HCS) phenotypes.
METHODS
A case-control (matched by age group and sex) study was conducted on regional cancer. Data on exposure factors and first-/second-degree family history of cancer (1/2FHC) were collected. The MGPT was performed using Illumina next-generation sequencing technology.
RESULTS
A total of 1,007 cases and 1,007 controls were included. The most frequent cancers were BC (n = 311), CCR (n = 147), prostate (n = 132), and lung cancers (n = 89). It was independently associated with cancer, 1/2FHC, tobacco consumption (TC), pesticide exposure (PE), solvent/glue exposure, and BMI <24. BC was associated with 1/2FHC, TC, and hormone replacement therapy use; CCR with 1/2FHC, TC, and BMI <24; prostate cancer with 1/2FHC, TC, and alcohol consumption; and lung cancer with 1/2FHC, TC, PE, and BMI <24. MGPT identified pathogenic/likely pathogenic mutations in 24 (32%) women with BC and in three (18%) women and four (24%) men diagnosed with CCR at under 50 years. Among the tested patients under 50 years with diagnosed BC and CCR, 98.6% and 97% present criteria for HCS, respectively.
CONCLUSION
This study confirmed the association of several factors associated with BC, CCR, prostate, and lung cancers and reinforced the importance of evaluating FHC and genetic testing, especially for patients under 50 years with diagnosed BC or CCR. A better understanding of population-specific cancer risk factors builds on sustainable data for developing prevention strategies. These efforts increase the commitment to early detection and surveillance.
Report of a matched case-control study to evaluate cancer risk factors, including molecular testing in Southern Brazil
INTRODUCTION
Cancer is a leading cause of death and affects the population and health systems. Moreover, its incidence is rising in middle-income developing mixed economy countries, such as Brazil. 1 , 2 In the past 15 years, the incidence has increased each year in Brazil, with rates from 472,050 new cancer cases in 2005 to 489,270 new cancer cases in 2010, 576,000 in 2015, and 625,000 each year from 2020 to 2022. 1 - 3 There are important disparities in cancer incidence and mortality between the country's five geographic regions. This variation may be due to different reasons, such as socioeconomic development, exposure to cancer risk factors (CRFs) and lifestyle, and the heterogeneous genetic makeup of the population. 1 , 4

Relevance
This study, developed in a middle-income country with high cancer rates in some areas, such as in the Southern Brazilian region, reinforced the importance of managing known cancer risk factors (CRF), evaluating cancer family history, and genetic testing for breast and colorectal cancers diagnosed in patients under 50 years. Understanding population-specific CRF builds on sustainable data for developing prevention strategies.
Cancer has a complex and multifactorial etiology with a strong interplay between genetic, demographic, hormonal, and environmental factors. 4 The magnitude of cancer burden attributable to potentially modifiable CRF was analyzed in the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study in 2019. According to this study, the leading risk factors at the most detailed level globally for risk attributable cancer deaths and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in 2019 for both sexes combined were smoking, followed by alcohol use and high BMI. Therefore, reducing exposure to these modifiable CRFs would decrease cancer mortality and DALY rates. 6
A few studies have been conducted in Brazil to screen for potential CRFs from different regions and evaluate different cancer types. 8 - 20 The genetic CRF for cancer was analyzed in several studies, especially from South and Southeast Brazil, investigating the prevalence of hereditary phenotypes or specific genes associated with a genetic predisposition, especially among individuals diagnosed with cancer and with a family history of the disease, or evaluating founder mutations. 21 - 27
National monitoring data on CRFs among the general Brazilian population are currently limited. For example, in Southern Brazil, there is no comprehensive study evaluating CRFs nor including germline multigene hereditary panel testing (MGPT) in patients diagnosed with cancer under 50 years or accessing the prevalence of hereditary phenotype in this age group with breast or colorectal cancers.
In this study, we sought to identify genetic (including molecular testing and evaluation of hereditary cancer criteria), demographic, hormonal, and environmental CRFs for high-incidence cancers in a large sample of cancer cases and controls from Southern Brazil.
METHODS
Study Design and Setting
A single-center, hospital-based, matched case-control study was conducted between March 2018 and March 2021 at the Hospital Tacchini (Bento Gonçalves). This institution is a regional cancer reference center, considered a UNACON (High Complexity Unit in Oncology), a reference for the Northeast region of the Southernmost State of Brazil (Rio Grande do Sul).
Participants and Sample Size
Cases were defined as individuals diagnosed with invasive cancer treated in the UNACON. Cases were invited to participate consecutively and randomly in five weekly attendance shifts during the study period. Controls were any individual without a cancer diagnosis matched by age group and sex, recruited from various settings, including companies from different regional sectors and community events. Invitations to participate were made through social networks to them. The controls self-reported not being diagnosed with cancer and were defined by patient-reported outcomes. All participants signed a written informed consent before recruitment during an interview applying a questionnaire by study researchers. No selection was made taking into account age or another variable. All patients were included according to their acceptance, and the study was finished when the sample size was reached. The sample size was calculated considering a significance level of 5%, power of 80%, an odds ratio (OR) of 3.2, and one control for each case (1:1). The prevalence of exposure was considered 2%, with an R 2 = 0.02 between the exposure variable and educational level. The sample size calculated was 995 cases and 995 controls.
Variables and Data Collection
Information on demographic characteristics, exposure factors, and family history of cancer (FHC) was obtained by applying a questionnaire (Data Supplement, Supplementary Material S3), making a family pedigree of FHC, and through chart review of medical evaluations during patients' visits to the UNACON. For the classification of hereditary cancer, syndromes were analyzed by the criteria for Hereditary Breast and Ovarian Cancers (HBOC), Breast and Colorectal (HBCC), Ly Fraumeni, Lynch, and Cowden. 28
Tobacco consumption (TC) was considered when the use is related to the present or the past, with at least 100 cigarettes in a lifetime. Alcohol consumption was considered when the participant reported alcoholic beverages (yes/no). Physical activity was considered when the participant said practicing regardless of the frequency. For exposures to pesticides, solvents, and glues, the report of use (yes/no) by the participant and the name/class of the product were considered. A use description was also considered for contraceptive use and hormone replacement therapy (HRT; yes/no).
Molecular Analysis
Criteria for Testing
Diagnosis of breast cancer (BC) and colorectal cancer (CCR) in patients under 50 years.
DNA Isolation
Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes using the FlexiGene DNA Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) and was quantified using the NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).
Library Preparation for Next-Generation Sequencing Analysis
Libraries were prepared according to the Hereditary Cancer MASTR guide (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA).
Amplicon-Based Gene Panel Protocol
Amplification of the entire coding region, including the intron-exon boundaries of the genes BARD1 , BRCA1 , BRCA2 , BRIP1 , RAD51C , RAD51D , TP53 , MRE11A , RAD50 , NBN , FAM175A , ATM , PALB2 , STK11 , MEN1 , PTEN , CDH1 , MUTYH , CHEK2 , BLM , XRCC2 , EPCAM , MLH1 , MSH6 , PMS2 , and MSH2 , was carried out using the BRCA Hereditary Cancer MASTR Plus assay kit (Agilent) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The genes analyzed and their association with BC and CCR and hereditary cancer syndromes are presented in Appendix Table A 1 .
Sequencing
Products were subsequently analyzed by next-generation sequencing (NGS) using the Illumina platform, MiSeq (Illumina, San Diego, CA), using a v3 sequencing kit (600-cycle) and a read depth of at least 30× per base. The results were analyzed by MASTR reporter data analysis software, including copy number variation detection. According to the American College of Medical Genetics guidelines, variables were classified into five categories. 29
Ethical Considerations
This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hospital Tacchini (CAAE number 85223818.1.1001.5305).
Statistical Analysis
Conditional logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate CRFs, measured by OR with 95% CIs. First, all types of cancer were included in the same model. After that, each cancer type (prostate, lung, colorectal, and breast) was analyzed separately. Bivariable analysis means each CRF is adjusted by educational level measured at years of education to eliminate possible confounding effects. All CRFs presenting P < .20 on bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable analysis that was also adjusted by educational level. Only those with P < .05 were kept in the final models. All statistical analyses were performed using the Survival R Package. 30
RESULTS
A total of 1,007 cancer cases and 1,007 controls were included in this study. All were residents of the Northeastern region of Rio Grande do Sul (Brazil). Fifty-five percent were women and 78.9% were older than 50 years. There was no difference between the groups regarding ethnicity; most were White. However, patients with cancer had a lower educational level than controls. Because of this, education is included in the statistical model as an adjustment factor. The sociodemographic characteristics of the individuals included are described in Table 1 . Among the cases, 311 were diagnosed with breast, 147 with colorectal, 132 with prostate, and 89 with lung cancers. A total of 322 were diagnosed with other cancer types (Appendix Table A 2 ).
Sociodemographic Characteristics Among Cases and Matched Controls
For the analysis including all cancers together, in bivariate analysis, CRFs associated with risk in the model adjusted only by education were first- or second-degree FHC (1/2FHC; OR, 6.7 [95% CI, 5.4 to 8.2]; P < .001); TC (OR, 9.5 [95% CI, 7.2 to 12.5]; P < .001); alcohol consumption (OR, 14.4 [95% CI, 8.0 to 25.0]; P < .001); pesticide exposure (OR, 4.5 [95% CI, 3.3 to 6.1]; P < .001); solvent/glue exposure (OR, 3.9 [95% CI, 2.3 to 6.5]; P < .001); and BMI (OR, 1.4 [95% CI, 1.1 to 1.7]; P = .01). In the multivariable analysis, CRFs independently associated with risk were 1/2FHC (OR, 6.1 [95% CI, 4.7 to 7.9]; P < .001); TC (OR, 8.8 [95% CI, 6.2 to 12.5]; P < .001); alcohol consumption (OR, 9.0 [95% CI, 4.3 to 19.1]; P < .001); pesticide exposure (OR, 2.9 [95% CI, 1.9 to 4.4]; P < .001); solvent/glue exposure (OR, 1.9 [95% CI, 1.0 to 4.3]; P = .04); and BMI <24 (OR, 1.5 [95% CI, 1.1 to 0.2.1]; P = .009; Table 2 ).
Associations Between Risk Factors and Cancer (overall and according to cancer types)
The use of pesticides was reported in 215 cases and 50 controls. Almost 12% reported using fungicides among the cases and 16.0% among the controls ( P < .001). The use of herbicides was reported in 27% of the cases and 32% of the controls ( P < .001). The use of insecticides for agriculture was reported in 1.9% of the cases and 2.0% of the controls. Among cases and controls, 24.6% and 24.0% reported using two or more pesticides. In addition, 34.9% of the cases and 26.0% of the controls did not know the name or the class of the pesticide (Table 3 ).
Pesticides Used by Cases and Controls Described by Chemical Types
BC
In the multivariable analysis, the following factors were independently associated with a higher risk of BC: 1/2FHC (OR, 6.2 [95% CI, 4.1 to 9.5]; P < .001); TC (OR, 4.2 [95% CI, 2.4 to 7.5]; P < .001); and HRT use (OR, 3.0 [95% CI, 1.2 to 7.6]; P = .02; Table 2 ). Alcohol consumption was not evaluated since nobody in the control group was exposed to this CRF.
CCR
The following factors were associated with CCR risk in the multivariable analysis: 1/2FHC (OR, 4.7 [95% CI, 2.6 to 8.6]; P < .001); TC (OR, 3.1 [95% CI, 1.5 to 6.3]; P = .002); and BMI <24 (OR, 2.1 [95% CI, 1.0 to 4.3]; P = .04; Table 2 ). Alcohol consumption was not evaluated since nobody in the control group was exposed to this CRF.
Prostate Cancer
The following factors were associated with prostate cancer risk in the multivariable analysis: 1/2FHC (OR, 6.7 [95% CI, 2.8 to 15.5]; P < .001); TC (OR, 10.5 [95% CI, 4.2 to 26.3]; P < .001); and alcohol consumption (OR, 7.3 [95% CI, 1.3 to 40.5]; P = .01; Table 2 ). Only one control was exposed to pesticides, making it impossible to analyze this CRF.
Lung Cancer
The following factors were associated with lung cancer risk in the multivariable analysis: 1/2FHC (OR, 30.2 [95% CI, 4.2 to 218.0]; P < .001); TC (OR, 1,331.9 [95% CI, 48.1 to 36,884.9]; P < .001); pesticides exposure (OR, 46.5 [95% CI, 4.1 to 528.2]; P = .002); and BMI <24 (OR, 9.3 [95% CI, 1.3 to 67.8]; P = .02). Physical activity conferred risk reduction (OR, 0.07 [95% CI, 0.01 to 0.54]; P = .009; Table 2 ).
Hereditary Cancer Risk Assessment
MGPT of 26 cancer predisposition genes identified germline pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in 24 (32%) women with BC: 11 (44%) in BRCA1/2 , 13 (52%) in non- BRCA genes, and one (4%) in a gene with a controversial association with BC predisposition (Tables 4 and 5 ).

Description of the Molecular Variants Identified
In addition, pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants were identified in three (18%) women and four (24%) men diagnosed with CCR: seven (70%) in genes associated with colorectal cancer predisposition, two (20%) and one (10%) in genes with controversial or unrelated association with CCR predisposition, respectively. At least one variant of uncertain significance (VUS) was identified in 20 (27%) women and one (50%) man diagnosed with BC and in four (24%) women and three (18%) men diagnosed with CCR.
Among BC cases tested, the median age at diagnosis was 39 (20-49) years, and 98.6% met at least one phenotypic criterion for hereditary cancer predisposition syndromes. Among women, 69% present criteria for HBOC, 28% for HBOC and Li Fraumeni syndromes, 1% for HBOC and Lynch syndromes, and 1% for Li Fraumeni syndrome. Among men, 50% present criteria for HBOC, and 50% present no criteria for hereditary cancer predisposition syndrome.
Among CCR cases tested, the median age at diagnosis was 46 (10-49) years, and 97% met at least one phenotypic criterion for hereditary cancer predisposition syndromes. Among women, 88% present criteria for Lynch syndrome, 6% for HBOC syndrome, and 6% present no criteria for any hereditary cancer predisposition syndrome. Among men, 6% present criteria for familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) syndrome, 6% for Li Fraumeni syndrome, and 88% for Lynch syndrome.
DISCUSSION
To our knowledge, this study is the first comprehensive, matched case-control analysis of CRF for common cancers conducted in Brazil. The study site is a regional health center where 66% of patients come from the public health care system in the far South of Brazil, known as Serra Gaúcha . 31 Therefore, the data from this study are important and present a picture of the principal CRF already consolidated in the literature for the most diagnosed cancers in the region, which are the same as those diagnosed nationally. 19
A better understanding of the local/regional CRFs may inform more effective prevention strategies. When CRFs are identified and well understood, health care providers can supply individuals with more accurate information on their disease risks and develop tailored risk-reducing strategies. In addition, these efforts increase the commitment of health providers and patients to early detection, allowing for more focused preventive health education and management. In Brazil, where 70%-80% of the population rely on public health care, this approach is relevant and needs to be mainly discussed. 7
Although many studies analyzing CRFs have been conducted, reported results vary widely. This variation may be related to disparities in study designs, geographical features, genetic background, and the specific populations' lifestyle and/or health care factors. Therefore, investigating and clarifying CRFs for the most commonly diagnosed cancers regionally is crucial. Furthermore, knowing manageable CRFs can help promote better prevention strategies and control, especially among countryside communities, commonly in this region of Brazil.
CRFs for breast, colorectal, or lung cancer cases were analyzed in some previous Brazilian case-control studies. For BC, it was associated with BMI ≥30 and postmenopausal status 9 ; family history of BC; history of BC among first-degree female relatives and oral contraceptive use 8 ; regular consumption of alcoholic beverages and sedentary behavior in premenopausal and postmenopausal women, respectively; current smoking or previous history of smoking in postmenopausal women 11 ; regular alcohol consumption 12 ; and living near cropland with pesticides. In addition, physical activity (past 6 months) was a protective factor against the disease. 10 Lung cancer was associated with risk for the disease in two studies: former TC and TC. 9 , 13
Our study associated premenopausal status with a higher risk of BC. Previous studies showed that postmenopausal women have a lower risk of BC than premenopausal women of the same age and childbearing pattern. 33 In addition, they consider that the risk increases by almost 3% each year after menopause onset (natural or surgery-induced). Therefore, women who attain menopause at age 55 years rather than 45 years have an approximately 30% higher risk. 32 , 33
HRT was associated with a higher risk of BC in our sample, confirming worldwide evidence from several studies that current and recent users of HRT were at increased risk for BC. 34 In addition, a recent study reinforced the importance of HRT as a risk factor for BC. It concluded that users of systemic HRT who started around the time of menopause were at greater risk of invasive BC than apparently similar never users. 35
In this study, BMI <24 was associated with a higher risk for colorectal and lung cancers. However, BMI <24 may likely be related to the disease process instead. Previous publications from this study center showed that 85.7% and 62.3% of lung and CCR, respectively, presented advanced or metastatic stages at diagnosis. 36 Epidemiologic studies suggest that the frequency of weight loss in patients with lung and CCR is present in 55%–60% of the cases. It is considered more common among those diagnosed with metastatic stage. 37 , 38 In this context, a special report of the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) group was described as inadequate owing to limited data, inconsistent results, or no data suggesting any association between BMI and lung cancer. 39
Alcohol consumption strongly correlates with developing oral cavity, pharynx, esophagus, stomach, colorectum, CNS, pancreatic, breast, and prostate cancers. It is shown that associations between alcohol consumption and prostate cancer are modest and complex. 40 Another study evaluating the association between alcohol consumption and lung cancer described a slightly increased risk of lung cancer associated with the consumption of ≥30 g of alcohol per day than with no alcohol consumption. Alcohol consumption was strongly associated with an increased risk of lung cancer in male never-smokers. 41
Organochlorine and organophosphorus pesticides have been investigated in oxidative stress induction and their potential role in cancer development and progression. 42 In our study, pesticides were associated with risk independently of the cancer type and lung cancers. 43 , 44
We assessed occupational exposure to solvents and glues containing aromatic hydrocarbons, and it was grouped with all cancer types. Several monocyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (benzene, toluene, xylene, and styrene), present in several types of solvents and glues, are classified as definite or possible carcinogens to humans. Two large case-control studies analyzing information on the lifelong work history of cases and controls found that occupational exposure to solvents was associated with higher risks of prostate and lung cancers. 45 , 46
Smoking was significantly associated with lung, prostate, and CCR incidence and mortality in several meta-analyses. 47 , 48 A meta-analysis of case-control and cohort studies on tobacco smoking and BC occurrence confirmed a moderate increase in the risk of BC in women who smoke tobacco. 50
The risk association for cancer in individuals presenting a positive 1/2FHC confirms previous findings from multiple studies worldwide. 51 The risks associated with a positive FHC are highly dependent on the number of affected relatives, the degree of relationship, and the age at which an affected relative was diagnosed. 52
The BC and CCR referred to MGPT in our study met at least one criterion for hereditary cancer predisposition syndromes, reinforcing the need for a detailed assessment of the FHC in this region for those diagnosed with cancer, mainly when the diagnosis occurs at a young age. Therefore, identifying a history of multiple cancers, especially at a young age, should prompt referral to maximize the potential for identifying those with hereditary cancer and consequent implementation of cancer risk-reducing measures.
Interestingly, the prevalence of germline pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants identified here seems higher than that observed in other international cohorts of patients with BC and CCR. This prevalence may be related to the early age at diagnosis criterion used for enrollment. 55 - 57 Another possibility would be that one or a few founders' genetic variants could be at play in the region determining most of the cancer burden. However, this was not observed for most of the genetic variants identified in the present cohort.
Additionally, the burden of heredity in the region proved to be relevant. Approximately one in three women with BC and one in five women or men with CCR diagnosed under 50 years had a pathogenic or likely pathogenic germline variant. Therefore, it is essential that genetic testing is performed in the context of genetic counseling to ensure that the information is effectively transmitted within families and also to address the issue of variants of uncertain significance, a common finding when using MGPT. Furthermore, a significant percentage of the individuals analyzed had a VUS, and current studies must be reassessed periodically to verify their role in the disease.
By 2040, the IARC projected the number of cancer cases in South America to increase by 76.5% and cancer-related deaths by 91.2%. 54 Among the CRFs analyzed here, several were manageable and may contribute to crucial preventive strategies to optimize cancer control in communities with difficulties or lack access to cancer care. Primary prevention and cancer screening programs are the most cost-effective means to reduce the burden of cancer in Latin America. 57
This study presents limitations: As the cases were from a single center, with a limited sample size for each tumor type, the clinical cancer staging was not collected to analyze its interactions with CRF. In addition, the controls were defined by patient-reported outcomes.
